4.3 — Internalizing Externalities

Chapter 4: Public Goods & Externalities

Two Main Solutions

When the market fails because of externalities, we have two main ways to fix it:

1. Private Solutions

Coase Theorem (Coase, 1960)

The market might fix itself through private negotiations without government help.

2. Public Solutions

The government intervenes using:

  • Price Regulation: Taxes & Subsidies.
  • Quantity Regulation: Quotas & Bans.
  • Marketable Permits: Cap-and-trade systems.

The Coase Theorem (Private Solution)

Coase asked: Are externalities really outside the market? Can private deals make the price reflect external costs?

Coase Theorem — Part I: If property rights are well-defined and bargaining is costless, then agents will negotiate to reach the socially optimal quantity.

Coase Theorem — Part II: The efficient outcome doesn't depend on who owns the rights. It only changes who gets the money (income distribution), not the final quantity.

Example: The River Pollution

  • Case 1: Swimmers own the river. Firms pay them for each unit of pollution. Payment = Marginal Damage (MD).
  • Case 2: Firms own the river. Swimmers pay firms to pollute less. Payment = Marginal Damage (MD).

➡️ Result: In both cases, the negotiation leads to the same optimal quantity.

Coase Theorem Graphically

How to read: The market starts at $Q_1$ (where $PMC = PMB$). Through negotiation, agents move to $Q^*$ (where $SMC = SMB$), which is the social optimum.

Why Coase often fails (Problems):

  1. The Assignment Problem: It's hard to define who owns what, especially with many people.
  2. Transaction Costs: Negotiating takes time and money. Coordination is hard for global issues (like climate change).

Public Solution: Price Regulation

When Coase fails, the state uses Price Regulation to change behavior by changing prices.

Pigouvian Tax (Pigou, 1920): A corrective tax set equal to the external marginal cost. It makes the firm's cost equal to the social cost ($PMC = SMC$).

Tax Effect: The tax shifts the supply curve up by the amount of the damage. This forces producers to reduce quantity from $Q_1$ to $Q^*$.

Fines & Sanctions: Similar to taxes but more indirect. Companies change methods to avoid paying high fines.

Public Solution: Quantity Regulation

Instead of prices, the state can directly mandate how much of an activity is allowed.

  • Output Regulation: Limits on the final quantity (e.g., fishing quotas, emission standards).
  • Input Regulation: Requirements on how to produce (e.g., mandating specific filters).
  • Bans: Zero quantity allowed (e.g., smoking bans in public places).

Price vs Quantity

In a perfect world, they are the same. In practice, Price is more flexible but Quantity is easier when we don't know the exact demand curves.

The Scope of Public Action

Governments reach the social optimum through sophisticated mechanisms:

  • Internal: Representative democracy, voting systems (to extract preferences), and cost-benefit analysis.
  • International: Global externalities (like climate change) require cooperation between entities like the EU, UN, and OECD.

Marketable Permits (Cap-and-Trade)

A combination of both: The government sets a Cap (quantity) but lets firms Trade (price).

How it works: Each firm gets a permit to emit. If a firm emits less, it can sell its permit to a firm that needs to emit more. This ensures the total pollution stays under the limit efficiently.

Limits: Lobbying, equity issues (who gets the permits first?), and "hotspots" where pollution is concentrated locally.

🧠

ULTRA RECAP

  • Coase: Market fixes it if rights are clear & bargaining is free.
  • Taxes: Correct through prices (Pigouvian).
  • Quotas: Correct through direct quantity control.
  • Permits: Efficient mix of quantity limits and market trading.
