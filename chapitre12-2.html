12.2 — Offshore Wealth & Final Lessons

Chapter 12: Tax Evasion — Financial Secrecy and the Core Principles

Evasion at the Top: Offshore Wealth

Standard random tax audits severely underestimate evasion at the very top of the income distribution. Why? Because the ultra-rich do not simply hide cash under a mattress; they use sophisticated, multi-layered offshore structures and pass-through businesses.

📈 The Inequality Link: Tax evasion is highly concentrated. Research shows that while the bottom 50% evade very little, the top 0.01% of richest households evade roughly 25% of their taxes.

Massive amounts of global wealth (equivalent to about 10% of world GDP) are held offshore. Because this wealth is hidden, economic data usually underestimates true global wealth inequality. Accounting for tax evasion pushes the wealth share of the top 1% significantly higher.

Financial Secrecy & Data Leaks

This massive scale of evasion is only possible because of Financial Secrecy. Jurisdictions offer regulatory arbitrage—rules designed to legally hide the true identity of asset owners.

The Problem

🕵️‍♂️ Opaque Ownership

Shell companies, trusts, and anonymous bank accounts make it impossible for home countries to tax the assets of their wealthy citizens.

The Metric

📊 Financial Secrecy Index

Ranks countries not just by how secretive their laws are, but by their total share of global offshore financial services. A highly secretive but tiny island matters less than a slightly less secretive but massive financial hub.

Leaks vs. Systematic Transparency

Historically, our best insights came from massive, forced data dumps like the Panama Papers. These leaks trigger massive deposit withdrawals from the exposed jurisdictions.

However, the structural policy solution is AIE (Automatic Information Exchange). When countries sign AIE agreements, banks automatically send account details to the owner's home tax authority. While AIE drastically reduces offshore deposits in compliant countries, money often shifts to "pretender" countries—those that exchange information on paper but maintain extreme ownership registration secrecy in practice.

The 8 Ultimate Lessons from Public Economics

As the final summary of the entire Public Finance course, memorize these fundamental principles. They connect everything you have studied, from externalities to pensions to tax evasion.

  1. The Benchmark Problem

    Markets fail, but so does government — the question is always "compared to what?" You cannot assume state intervention is magically perfect. You must weigh market failure against government failure.

  2. The Eternal Trade-off

    Every policy is a trade-off between equity and efficiency. There is no free lunch, but some lunches are cheaper than others (e.g., broad tax bases with low rates create less deadweight loss).

  3. Statutory vs. Economic Incidence

    Who writes the check is not who bears the cost. Tax incidence depends entirely on elasticities, not on who the law targets. The inelastic side of the market always pays.

  4. Incentives vs. Intentions

    Good intentions don't survive bad incentives. Moral appeals fail if the financial reward for breaking the rules is massive. System design matters more than hoping people will "do the right thing."

  5. The Race to the Bottom

    Capital moves to where it's treated best. In a globalized world, countries competing for mobile capital by slashing corporate rates often harms overall welfare and public revenues.

  6. Evasion Drives Inequality

    Tax evasion is not random — it is concentrated at the top. It makes actual wealth inequality much higher than official statistics suggest, often invisibly.

  7. Information > Audits

    Information is the most powerful enforcement tool. Third-party reporting (employers reporting wages, banks reporting interest) is far more effective at stopping evasion than random audits or harsh penalties.

  8. Secrecy is a Choice

    Transparency is a policy. Secrecy is also a policy. The existence of offshore tax havens is not a law of nature; it is the result of deliberate legislative choices made by specific jurisdictions.
