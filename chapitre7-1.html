Chapter 7: Health Economics — Theory, Systems, and the Cost of Aging

Why Does Healthcare Need Its Own Economics?

Healthcare markets violate the standard assumptions required for the First Welfare Theorem to hold. Unlike ordinary commodity markets, healthcare is characterised by uncertainty (you do not know when or whether you will fall ill), information asymmetry (physicians know far more than patients), and externalities (vaccination benefits everyone, not just the vaccinated individual).

Kenneth Arrow's seminal 1963 paper demonstrated that healthcare is fundamentally different from normal goods, and that the standard welfare theorems break down when these key market conditions fail. This is why governments intervene so heavily in health markets across the world.

Arrow (1963) — Why Health Markets Fail

Arrow's 1963 paper in the American Economic Review remains the foundational analysis of why healthcare markets cannot function like competitive commodity markets. He identifies five key market failures:

🎲 Uncertainty

Illness is fundamentally unpredictable. Consumers cannot assess provider quality ex ante, and do not know when they will need care. This violates the standard assumption that consumers have complete information about what they are buying.

🔍 Information Asymmetry

Physicians know far more than patients about diagnosis, treatment options, and prognosis. This creates an agency problem: the doctor acts both as the advisor (telling you what you need) and the supplier (selling it to you).

🌍 Externalities

Vaccination, infectious disease control, and public hygiene create social benefits far beyond the individual receiving care. These positive externalities mean that private markets systematically under-provide these services relative to the social optimum.

🏥 Non-Competitive Structure

Licensing requirements, patent protections, geographic monopolies (especially in rural areas), and high entry barriers limit competition. Healthcare providers often face little competitive pressure, enabling prices well above marginal cost.

⚠️ Important Clarification: Healthcare is NOT a public good! It is easily excludable (you can deny treatment to someone who does not pay) and it is rival (a hospital bed used by one patient cannot be used by another). However, it exhibits so many other market failures that standard competitive outcomes are still inefficient. If health care is so difficult for economists, maybe we can try analysing health instead → this is the Grossman approach.

Arrow, K.J. (1963). Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care. AER, 53(5).

The Grossman Model (1972) — Health as Durable Capital

Michael Grossman's 1972 model represents a radical departure from standard demand theory. The key insight is that people do not demand healthcare for its own sake — they demand health. Medical care is merely an input that produces health. In other words, the demand for medical care is a derived demand.

Health is modelled as a durable capital stock (analogous to physical capital like machinery) that produces a flow of "healthy time". This healthy time has value in two ways:

🛋️ Consumption Good

Healthy time enters the utility function directly — you feel better when you are healthy. Being sick reduces your well-being regardless of income. This is the consumption motive for investing in health.

💼 Investment Good

Healthy time enables work and earnings — being ill means lost wages and productivity. Health capital generates a monetary return analogous to physical or human capital. This is the investment motive.

Grossman Model — The Formal Framework

The individual maximises lifetime utility subject to health dynamics and a budget constraint. Utility depends on both the health stock (you feel better when healthy) and on income/consumption.

Objective Function:

$U = U\bigl[u_t(c_t, H_t);\; u_{t+1}(c_{t+1}, H_{t+1});\; \ldots;\; u_n(c_n, H_n)\bigr]$

where $c_t$ = consumption and $H_t$ = health stock at time $t$.

Health Dynamics — The Capital Accumulation Equation:

$H_{t+1} = H_t + I_t - \delta_t H_t$

= current stock + gross investment − depreciation

Investment Function:

$I_t = I(M_t, TH_t;\; E_i)$

where $M$ = medical care, $TH$ = own time devoted to health, and $E_i$ = education, environment and other individual characteristics.

The depreciation rate $\delta_t$ increases with age — this is how the model captures biological aging. As you get older, your body deteriorates faster, and maintaining the same health stock requires increasingly costly investment.

Solving the Model — The Equilibrium Condition

Following human capital theory, individuals invest in themselves (e.g. through training or education) to increase their productivity. The optimal amount of investment is determined by comparing costs and benefits — costs mostly occur in the short-term, while benefits accrue in the future in the form of enhanced income and wellness.

The Optimisation Problem:

$\max \sum_{t} \delta^t \, U(H_t, Y_t)$

subject to:

$H_t = H_{t-1} + I_{t-1} - \delta_t \, H_{t-1}$

and income equation:

$Y_t = p_t \cdot w_t + (1 - p_t) \cdot b_t$

where $p_t$ = probability of being healthy, $w_t$ = wage when healthy, and $b_t$ = income when ill (e.g. sick leave benefit).

The Equilibrium Condition (Grossman, 1972):

The solution is that the marginal cost of investing in health capital equals the marginal benefit:

$r + \delta = w \times \frac{\text{marginal product of health investment}}{\text{direct cost of investment in health}}$

  • Marginal cost (left-hand side) = the interest rate $r$ plus the health depreciation rate $\delta$. This is the user cost of health capital — analogous to the rental price of a machine.
  • Marginal benefit (right-hand side) = the ratio of wages to the cost paid for the extra unit of health. Higher wages call for higher investment in health, because being sick costs you more in lost earnings.
  • The marginal product of health investment increases as health diminishes — when your health stock is very low, a small investment yields a large improvement. Conversely, at high health levels, diminishing returns set in.
  • Direct cost may decrease (vaccination — cheap, high return) or increase (brain surgery — expensive, complex) depending on the type of care.

The graph shows the optimal health stock $H^*$ where the downward-sloping MEC curve (marginal efficiency of health capital, $\frac{wG(H)}{C(H)}$) intersects the horizontal user cost line ($r + \delta$). At the intersection (point X), marginal benefit = marginal cost, determining the optimal level of health capital.

Grossman Model — Comparative Statics

The model generates several testable predictions about how changes in economic conditions affect the demand for health and healthcare:

Change Mechanism Prediction
↑ Age $\delta_t$ rises → user cost $(r + \delta_t)$ increases → lower optimal $H^*$ Health declines with age — maintaining health becomes increasingly expensive as the depreciation rate rises.
↑ Education Higher efficiency of health investment → lower effective price of producing health → higher $H^*$ Education-health gradient — more educated individuals are healthier, partly because they produce health more efficiently.
↑ Price of care Higher cost of the health input $M$ → investment in health becomes more expensive → lower $H^*$ Demand is downward-sloping — as the price of healthcare rises, individuals invest less in health.
↑ Wages Higher opportunity cost of being sick → higher marginal benefit of health → higher $H^*$ Income-health gradient — higher earners invest more because illness costs them more in foregone earnings.
💀 Key Insight: Death occurs when $H$ falls below the minimum threshold $H_{\min}$ — the model endogenises lifespan. A person "dies" when their health stock has depreciated to the point where maintaining it above the survival threshold is no longer worth the cost.

Evaluating the Grossman Model

The Grossman model formalises a fairly intuitive process — people invest in their own health, and this investment is subject to the usual cost-benefit logic of capital theory.

Key Insights and Strengths:

  • Maintaining good health requires more resources as we get older, because the opportunity cost of maintaining health increases with the depreciation rate $\delta_t$.
  • Individuals who earn higher wages will invest more in their health, because being sick is more costly for them in terms of lost earnings.
  • The rate of depreciation $\delta_t$ is critically important — it is the key parameter that determines the life-cycle pattern of health investment.
  • Despite its limitations, the Grossman model remains the foundational demand-side model in health economics and is still the starting point for most theoretical work in the field.

Limitations:

  • Assumes rational, forward-looking agents — but health decisions are often emotional, habitual, or poorly informed.
  • Ignores supplier-induced demand — doctors can shift the patient's demand curve (information asymmetry).
  • Treats health production as a deterministic process — in reality, the relationship between medical inputs and health outcomes is highly uncertain.

Unusual Features of Health Demand

Several empirical features of health demand set it apart from other goods:

  • Derived demand: People demand health, not healthcare per se — this is the Grossman insight in action.
  • Supplier-induced demand: Physicians can shift the patient's demand curve because of the information asymmetry — a doctor who is paid per procedure can recommend more procedures than strictly necessary.
  • Price elasticity: Demand is relatively inelastic ($-0.2$), particularly for emergency and inpatient care. Higher elasticity is observed for outpatient and preventive care, where patients have more discretion.
  • Income elasticity paradox: At the country level, health spending rises more than proportionally with GDP ($>1.0$), making it appear a luxury good. At the individual level, elasticity is near 1.0 ($\approx 1.0$), making it a normal good. This aggregation paradox arises because richer countries also tend to adopt more expensive medical technologies.

Chapter 7.1 — The Grossman Model:

  • Arrow (1963): Healthcare markets fail because of uncertainty, information asymmetry, externalities, and non-competitive structure. But healthcare is NOT a public good.
  • Grossman (1972): People demand health, not healthcare. Health is a durable capital stock whose depreciation rate $\delta_t$ increases with age.
  • Equilibrium: $r + \delta = w \times \frac{MP_H}{C_H}$ — invest in health until marginal cost (user cost of capital) equals marginal benefit (wages × marginal product / cost).
  • Comparative statics: health declines with age, rises with education and wages, falls with healthcare prices. Death = $H < H_{\min}$.
  • Health demand is price-inelastic ($-0.2$) and exhibits an income elasticity paradox (luxury at country level, normal at individual level).
