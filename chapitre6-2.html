Chapter 6: The Economics of Old-Age Pensions

Measuring Population Ageing

The single most important statistic for pension economics is the Old-Age Dependency Ratio (OADR). It tells us how many retired people each working-age person must support.

Definition:

$\text{OADR} = \frac{\text{Population aged 65+}}{\text{Population aged 15–64}} \times 100$

Intuitively: how many retired persons must each working-age person support?

Why It Matters for Pensions

In a Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG) system, current workers' contributions directly finance current pensioners' benefits. A rising dependency ratio means fewer contributors per beneficiary, squeezing the financial balance of the system.

Historical Context

When Germany introduced Bismarck's state pension in 1889, life expectancy barely reached the retirement age of 70. Today, a 65-year-old German can expect to live approximately 20 additional years, fundamentally altering the financial maths of any pension promise.

Fertility Rates, 1960–2023

Fertility is the long-term driver of demographic ageing. Fewer births today means fewer workers to fund pensions in 20–30 years.

Empirical Trends (Source: Eurostat):

  • In the 1960s, EU countries had on average around 2+ children per woman. Today, most have fallen to 1.3–1.5 — well below the replacement level of 2.1 needed to keep population stable.
  • There is significant variation within Europe: France remains relatively higher (around 1.6–1.8 recently), while Southern and Eastern Europe (Italy, Spain, Czech Republic) often fall to 1.2–1.4.
  • A slight recovery occurred in the 2000s, but a new decline after 2010 has confirmed the structural nature of low fertility across the continent.
⚠️ Key Fact: Persistent low fertility = structural demographic ageing. This is not a temporary trend — it is a permanent shift in the population structure that makes PAYG systems increasingly difficult to sustain.

📈 Key Takeaway: The EU-27 dependency ratio nearly doubles over the projection period (from 33% to 57%). This places enormous strain on existing pension arrangements and makes reform — whether through higher retirement ages, lower benefits, or higher contributions — unavoidable.

Does Ageing Drive Spending? The Cross-Country Evidence

Is there a clear relationship between how old a country's population is and how much it spends on pensions?

Empirical Finding: Plotting the old-age dependency ratio against pension spending (% of GDP) across European economies and the US reveals a moderate positive correlation ($R^2 \approx 0.53$). Countries with older populations tend to spend more — but the relationship is far from deterministic.

The Japan Outlier: Japan has an OADR of ~49% but spends only ~9% of GDP on pensions. This is far below what the regression line would predict. The explanation lies in institutional differences — Japan's NDC-style reforms, later retirement ages, and a cultural norm of continued work into old age all bring spending below the European pattern.

💡 Key Insight: Ageing matters, but institutions also strongly matter. A country with aggressive pension reform (Japan, Sweden) can spend significantly less than what its demographics would suggest, while a country with a generous unreformed system (Italy, France) will spend far more.

What drives the divergence?

  • Demographic pressure: The sharp rise in the dependency ratio mechanically raises expenditure, particularly in the 2025–2045 window when the baby-boom cohort retires across Europe.
  • Reform effects: Many EU states have legislated increases in retirement ages, tightened early-exit options, or shifted to notional defined-contribution (NDC) formulas, moderating long-run spending relative to the no-reform baseline.
  • Country heterogeneity: Italy's spending falls from 15.5% to 13.7% of GDP thanks to the 1995 Dini reform, whereas France remains above 12% throughout, reflecting gentler parametric adjustment.

Chapter 6.2 — The Demographic Challenge:

  • The Old-Age Dependency Ratio is the single most important metric for pension sustainability. It is currently ~33% in the EU and ~49% in Japan.
  • Fertility has fallen well below replacement level (2.1) across almost all of Europe, making demographic ageing a permanent structural reality.
  • The OADR is projected to nearly double in the EU by 2070 (from 33% to 57%), making pension reform unavoidable.
  • Pension spending varies hugely (Italy 16% vs US 7%), reflecting different systems and reform histories. Ageing matters, but institutions also matter — Japan is the key outlier.
