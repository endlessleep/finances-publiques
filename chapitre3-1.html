3.1 — Fiscal Dominance

Chapter 3: Fiscal-Monetary Policy Nexus

What is Fiscal Dominance?

Definition

Fiscal dominance happens when a country's fiscal policy (government spending and borrowing) controls or limits its monetary policy (central bank actions). This makes it very hard for the central bank to manage inflation and keep prices stable.

In short, fiscal dominance is a situation where government debt pressures force the central bank to put the government's needs before its own goals. This often leads to inflation and hurts economic stability.

In a healthy economy, the central bank sets interest rates on its own to control inflation, while the government manages its spending and tax money responsibly. Fiscal dominance breaks this rule: when the government spends or borrows too much, the central bank has no choice but to help — usually by printing money (monetization) or keeping interest rates artificially low — to avoid a massive debt crisis.

This is one of the most important ideas where monetary and fiscal policy meet, and it causes terrible problems in the real world: hyperinflation in Zimbabwe, the European debt crisis in 2010-2012, and even the recent post-Covid inflation spike can all be explained using the idea of fiscal dominance.

The Dynamic Budget Constraint

To really understand fiscal dominance, we need to look at the government's dynamic budget constraint — the math formula that shows how public debt grows over time.

Key Equation — Discrete Time

$b_{t+1} = \frac{d_t - \mu_t h_t}{(1+\pi_t)(1+g_t)} + \frac{1+r_t}{1+g_t} b_t$

Key Equation — Continuous Time (Simplified)

$\dot{b}_t \equiv d_t - \mu_t h_t + (r_t - g_t)b_t$

Variable Meaning
$b_t$ Debt-to-GDP ratio at time $t$ (how much debt compared to the economy size)
$d_t$ Primary deficit (government spending minus tax money, excluding interest payments)
$\mu_t h_t$ Seigniorage — money the central bank creates by printing cash ($\mu_t$ = money growth rate, $h_t$ = monetary base / GDP)
$\pi_t$ Inflation rate (how fast prices rise)
$g_t$ Real GDP growth rate (how fast the economy grows)
$r_t$ Real interest rate on government debt (how expensive it is to borrow)

The most important part is the term $(r_t - g_t)$ — the difference between the interest rate and the economy's growth rate. This creates the snowball effect:

Case 1

📈 When $r > g$

The borrowing interest rate is higher than country growth → the debt grows faster than the economy. This is the explosive path: even if the government stops spending too much, the debt keeps growing automatically because interest payments grow faster than GDP.

To survive this, budget deficits must be paid by printing money (seigniorage). Otherwise, the debt explodes completely.

Case 2

📉 When $r < g$

The country grows faster than the interest rate → the government has a soft budget limit. The debt size can safely calm down even if the government has deficits and doesn't print money, because fast GDP growth naturally "shrinks" the existing debt size.

This is the good, lucky situation. Darby (1983) called it "Some Pleasant Monetarist Arithmetic" — in real life, $r < g$ often magically lasts for many years.

💡 What can be fixed? When $r > g$, the government only has two realistic choices: print more money for seigniorage (forcing the central bank to print cash → causing inflation) or cut the primary deficit (by raising taxes or firing state workers). Fiscal dominance happens when the government refuses to fix the deficit and forces the central bank to print money instead.

The Four Policy Regimes

How do central banks and governments work together? Famous economists (Sargent & Wallace 1981, Leeper 1991, Sims 1994) proved that their interaction can be organized into four different situations (regimes). This depends on whether each policy is "active" (doing its own job strongly) or "passive" (giving up and helping the other).

✅ The Perfect Case

Monetary Policy Active + Fiscal Policy Passive

The dream situation in modern economics. The central bank actively fights to keep prices stable by moving interest rates (Taylor rule). The government passively changes taxes and spending to keep debt safely under control.

Result: stable prices and safe debt. This is how rich Western countries try to run their governments.

⚠️ Fiscal Dominance

Monetary Policy Passive + Fiscal Policy Active

The government stubbornly refuses to fix its taxes or spending. It keeps borrowing huge money without planning to pay it back. The central bank is forced to print money and surrender its rules just to save the government from failing.

Result: inflation is caused by bad government debt, not by the central bank. The central bank totally loses control over shop prices.

❓ Total Confusion

Monetary Policy Passive + Fiscal Policy Passive

Neither side takes command. Nobody controls the money properly. Many crazy outcomes can happen, and inflation is completely random — the country can drift into any price nightmare without anything stopping it.

💣 Explosive War

Monetary Policy Active + Fiscal Policy Active

Both sides try to crush the other. The central bank raises interest rates to kill inflation, while the government violently keeps spending borrowed money. These fighting actions create massive chaos and can end in an economic explosion.

The Core Logic: The 2×2 box of policy situations always asks one simple question: who surrenders to whom? In the perfect textbook case, the government surrenders to the central bank — the government raises taxes to keep debt safe, so the central bank is free to fight inflation. In fiscal dominance, the roles are flipped: the central bank is forced to print cash to cover government mistakes, illegally abandoning its inflation job. The two "bad" regimes show why clear laws about who is the boss are necessary for a stable country.

Why is Fiscal Dominance Bad?

Fiscal dominance is not just a teacher's theory — it has created some of the worst human disasters in financial history. There are four main ways this dominance destroys an economy:

Consequence 1

Hyperinflation — The Absolute Worst Outcome

When the central bank must wildly print money to pay government bills, this can — in terrible but very real historical cases — lead to hyperinflation (Cagan, 1956). The entire money system dies: prices double every few days, paper money becomes trash, and the country goes back to trading cows for bread.

Famous historical examples: Germany (1923), Zimbabwe (2008), Venezuela (2017-present), and Hungary (1946 — the worst hyperinflation ever recorded in human history).

Consequence 2

Currency Crises

Fiscal dominance can break a country's fixed exchange rate and cause the national money to collapse. Krugman (1979) made a famous model explaining this: the central bank tries to keep its money value steady, but must also print money for the greedy government. This forces the bank to slowly burn through its foreign dollar reserves until none are left, causing an unstoppable currency crash.

Central Bank Balance Sheet (Krugman Model)
Assets (What it Owns) Liabilities (What it Owes)
Foreign Dollar reserves ($e \cdot B_{F,t}$) Monetary Base / Printed Money ($M_{0,t}$)
Government bonds ($B_{H,t}$)

As the central bank buys more government debt to save the state, it must heavily sell its foreign dollars to keep its own currency strong. When the dollar reserves disappear → the currency peg dies → the currency crashes.

Consequence 3

Inability to Stop Inflation — "Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic"

Sargent & Wallace (1981) proved a confusing truth: if a country's money printing is controlled by government debt needs (with $r > g$), then trying to strictly print less money today will accidentally cause extreme inflation in the future.

Why? Because fighting inflation today forces the government to borrow more expensive debt instead of getting printed cash → this debt grows huge → demanding an even bigger explosion of printed money later. And if citizens are smart, they will panic expecting this future disaster, causing massive inflation immediately today.

Consequence 4

Self-Fulfilling Debt Crises

Darby (1983) noted that $r < g$ often lasts for a very long time — "Some Pleasant Monetarist Arithmetic". But weak governments always abuse such lucky times. Neither growth ($g$) nor interest ($r$) are actually controlled by the government:

  • Growth can easily turn negative during global recessions.
  • If government debt gets too scary, bankers demand higher risk fees, instantly causing $r > g$.
  • This triggers a psychological panic cycle: investors fear the country will fail → they demand huge interest rates → the huge rates make the debt impossible to pay → which proves their initial fear was right.
🚨 Real-world example: During the 2010-2012 European debt crisis, the borrowing interest rates for weak European countries skyrocketed. Greece hit ~30%, Portugal ~15%, Ireland ~14%, and Italy ~7%. These were classic panic spirals: high interest → terrible debt → even higher interest. Only the Head of the European Bank stepping in and yelling "whatever it takes" finally killed the panic.

The Solution: Banning Financial Cheating

How do smart, modern countries block fiscal dominance? They build strict legal walls:

  1. Central bank independence

    Central banks must be completely separated from the government — both politically (presidents cannot give them orders) and financially (they don't need government money to survive). They must have a clear, absolute mission to keep prices stable over anything else.

  2. Strict laws banning money printing for the state

    The European Union guarantees this in Article 123 of its core treaty: The central bank is legally prohibited from permanently lending money or buying debt directly from any government or politician.

  3. Money printing as an accident, not a tool

    Money printing (seigniorage) is treated simply as a tiny side-effect to strictly keep the central bank running independently, but it is kept incredibly low (usually less than 0.3% of the country's total money) in modern economies.

⚠️ Ideal vs. Reality: In a perfect university book, the absolute best solution would be beautiful teamwork between the bank and the state. But in the real dirty world, the second-best solution of brutally independent central banks is chosen. Why? Because teamwork requires deep trust, and greedy politicians will always try to bully the central bank into paying for their expensive election promises.

Fiscal dominance = the government bullies the central bank. It begins when the state refuses to fix its bad spending and violently forces the central bank to print money to save it. The math formula proves that when interest is higher than growth ($r > g$), this always ends in deadly debt explosions or hyperinflation. The only cure is strict laws: fully independent central banks that care only about price stability, backed by hard laws banning state funding.
