2.3 — Inequality Across Countries & Global Inequality

Chapter 2: Inequality

Inequality Across Countries

How does inequality look across the world? The two graphs below prove that inequality levels are completely different between countries — even between countries that are equally rich.

The top picture ranks countries by their Gini score (using disposable income). At the best end: Slovenia, Slovakia, Czech Republic, and Northern European countries — all scoring around 0.25–0.28. At the worst end: the USA (~0.39), Mexico, Chile, Costa Rica, and South Africa (the absolute highest, passing 0.60).

The bottom picture shows the identical list of countries using the 90/10 ratio. The message is exactly the same: in Northern Europe, a rich person (top 10%) makes roughly 3 times what a poor person (bottom 10%) makes. In Latin America and South Africa, this difference can jump to 10 or 15 times more.

💡 Key takeaway: Inequality is not a fixed law of nature — it changes enormously across countries that have similar money levels. This proves that policy choices (taxes, social help, labor rules) have a gigantic power over how fair a country becomes.

Income vs. Wealth Inequality

Cross-Country Comparison

This graph charts each country by its top 10% income (X) vs. top 10% wealth (Y). The big lesson: wealth inequality is always worse than income inequality. In the USA, the top 10% earns about 45% of all incomes but owns around 70% of all properties and money. In South Africa, both numbers are terrible.

Why? Because wealth piles up over the years through saving, houses getting more expensive, and family inheritance — things that heavily reward people who already have money.

Income-Wealth Heatmap

This colored chart shows how a person's income rank matches their wealth rank. The heavy diagonal line proves: people getting high salaries usually own expensive assets too.

But the rule isn't perfect: some people get a great salary but have no saved wealth (young workers with big school debts) and others have huge wealth but tiny salaries (retired grandparents living in a massive paid-off house). This is why both measures are needed to see the full picture of a person's life.

Long-Run Inequality Trends

This chart follows the Gini score in different countries since 1960. The stories are very unique: the USA shows inequality growing without stopping since 1980; France remains mostly flat and much lower; South Africa maintains dangerously high inequality the entire time.

Looking further into history — over 100 years — we observe the top decile income share (the slice of the pie taken by the top 10% richest people) across many countries. A classic pattern is visible in the West: ① huge inequality before 1940 → ② massive drop after World War II (thanks to heavy progressive taxes, destruction of rich factories in the war, and new welfare rules) → ③ going back up again since roughly 1980.

The comparison between France and the USA is brilliant. Before 1940, both were painfully unequal. After the war, both became much fairer. But since 1980, their paths split: American inequality exploded upwards (taxes on the rich were cut, banks got more freedom, worker unions died), while France only saw a tiny bump (because it protected its social sharing rules). This proves that the post-1980 rise in inequality was not destiny — it was a deliberate choice.

The Great Gatsby Curve

The Great Gatsby Curve is one of the most famous discoveries in modern economics. It tests the Gini score (X-axis) against social mobility (Y-axis) for dozens of countries. The truth: there is a strong negative connection — countries with high inequality have terrible social mobility.

🇩🇰 Nordic Countries

Low inequality and excellent mobility. The money your dad makes has a very weak influence on your future success. The real "land of dreams and chances" is actually Scandinavia.

🇺🇸 United States

High inequality and terrible mobility. Even though people talk about the "American Dream", the data proves that money operates like genetics there. If you are born poor in America, you will almost certainly stay poor, much more than in Denmark or Sweden.

The Real Story: The Great Gatsby Curve completely destroys the famous argument that "inequality is okay if people can climb the ladder." The math says the exact opposite: inequality destroys the ladder. The reasons are obvious: in highly unequal places, getting a good school, a good doctor, and good connections depends completely on your parents' wallet. These family advantages grow bigger in every generation, making your birth location — not your hard work — the ultimate judge of your life. This is the exact reason why the distribution branch of government (Musgrave) is needed.

The Impact of Public Policy: Market vs. Disposable Income

This image offers the strongest proof that government actions work. It compares the Gini score for market income (before the state does anything) to the Gini for disposable income (after taxes and social help) in various nations.

The big lessons:

  • Market income Gini is bad almost everywhere — even in Germany, Canada, and the USA, the raw capitalism inequality is huge before the state steps in.
  • Germany and Canada score massive improvements by using strong progressive taxes and giving generous money to the weak.
  • The USA scores a very poor improvement — its tax system is weak on the rich and its social help is notoriously light.

The distance between the market Gini and the disposable Gini is the true measure of government effort. A big distance = a strong caring state. A small distance = weak action.

Main government weapons against inequality:

  • Progressive income tax (if you make more, your tax rate goes higher)
  • Special tax discounts for very poor workers
  • Treating investing money differently than hard labor money
  • Taxes on massive family inheritances
  • Social safety nets (pensions, jobless aid)
  • Free schools (making the starting line equal)

Global Inequality

Two Pieces

Global inequality can be sliced into two completely different parts:

  • Within-country inequality: Differences among people inside the exact same border (e.g., Paris rich vs. Paris poor).
  • Between-country inequality: The inequality that would survive if every single person inside a country made the exact same money, but the countries had different global averages (e.g., comparing a standard Norwegian salary to a Nigerian salary).

The global Gini score climbed heavily from 1820 to roughly 1990, as Western factory countries sprinted far ahead of the rest of the planet. Recently, it has dropped just a bit, entirely because of the massive economic explosions in China and India. But global inequality is still frighteningly high — the world Gini sits around 0.65–0.70, performing much worse than the most unequal individual country on Earth.

This graph, using the Theil index math trick, shows a shocking shift: between-country inequality is shrinking (thanks to Asian factories), but within-country inequality is growing fast. The reality: today, global inequality is heavily driven by poor people fighting rich people inside their own countries, rather than poor countries fighting rich countries.

The Elephant Curve

The Elephant Curve (Lakner & Milanovic, 2016) is probably the most famous picture in modern economics. It presents the total money growth between 1988 and 2008 for every single rank of the entire global population's income. It beautifully looks like an elephant — with a tall back, a low dip, and a shooting trunk — and it reveals exactly who won and who died in globalization:

🐘 The Back

Global Middle Class (P20–P60)

The absolute champions of globalization: Asian middle classes, mostly in China and India, who saw their incomes explode (~60-80% increase). Hundreds of millions of people escaped terrible poverty.

🐘 The Dip

Western Middle Class (P70–P90)

The victims of globalization: working and normal-class people in rich countries (USA, Europe), who received almost zero income growth. Their factory jobs were shipped away, taken by robots, or destroyed by cheap foreign labor.

🐘 The Trunk

Global Top 1%

The other massive winners: the world's super-rich, who stole a gigantic slice of global money through rising stocks, banking power, and international business. Their bank accounts multiplied wildly.

This second picture hits even harder: it strictly measures who took the new global money. The top 1% and top 5% stole a completely unfair amount of the total new wealth compared to their tiny population size. Global creation of wealth was absolutely not shared nicely — it almost exclusively went to the new Asian workers and the untouchable global rich.

The newer version looks at two time windows (1988–2008 compared to 2008–2018), proving that the elephant shape is relaxing a little: growth has become slightly more fair and less crazily focused on the top 1%, with a healthier global middle class stabilizing. But the core truth has not changed: three groups remain (A — Asian winners; B — Western losers; C — rich elite winners).

🚨 Why this changes politics forever: The Elephant Curve mathematically proves why angry populist politics (like Brexit or Trump) exploded in the West. The frozen Zone B (P70–P90) represents genuine economic anger — and it creates huge voting power demanding border closures, heavy taxes, and the death of globalization. Understanding this graph is the key to designing government taxes that angry people will actually tolerate.

Key takeaways from Chapter 2:

  • We care about inequality for instrumental reasons (it ruins society) and intrinsic reasons (it's legally unfair).
  • The Kuznets Curve (upside-down U shape) is old history — recent numbers show an N-shape where inequality comes back.
  • A solid inequality formula must pass strict tests: Transfer Principle, Anonymity, Scale Invariance, Decomposability.
  • The Gini coefficient (drawn from the Lorenz curve) is the global favorite, but Atkinson and GE math give special benefits.
  • Income can be counted as Market, Gross, Disposable, or Extended — each gives a wildly different story about inequality.
  • Wealth is impossibly more unequal than simple salary. The Great Gatsby Curve proves that high inequality destroys your chances of moving up in life.
  • Government laws (taxes + social money) successfully crush inequality — but some countries work much harder at it than others.
  • Across the whole earth, the gap between countries is shrinking, but the gap between neighbors is growing.
  • The Elephant Curve exposed globalization's champions (Asian workers + top 1% kings) and the angry losers (Western middle class).
