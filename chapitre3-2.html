3.2 — Is There Fiscal Dominance in Czechia?

Chapter 3: Fiscal-Monetary Policy Nexus

The Context: Post-Covid Inflation

Prices in Czechia went up faster than in most other European countries between 2019 and 2024. At the same time, the government's financial health became much worse. This made people ask: how much of this inflation was caused by bad government spending? Could this be a fiscal dominance problem?

FTPL — Fiscal Theory of the Price Level

The recent huge inflation made economists look again at the Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (FTPL). Normal money theory says inflation is always caused by printing too much cash. But the FTPL argues differently: it says that the country's price level is directly controlled by the government's long-term debt problems.

"The main idea of the FTPL is that the value of money is balanced against the government's ability to pay off its debts using future taxes."

Economists Barro & Bianchi (2023) think that high government spending is exactly why the inflation explosion in Czechia was so terrible. But is this theory actually true? Let's look at the real facts.

Covid-Related Budget Deficits & Recovery

Period Financial Health Main Causes
2020 Government money crashed very fast Covid sickness + huge emergency spending (lockdown money, paying workers to stay home)
2021–2022 Recovery totally stopped 2022 Russian war in Ukraine + crazy electricity prices; the state had to spend even more to help
2023 Slowly getting better The government started fixing the debt, but much slower than in the past (like 2012)
2024+ Good results from the savings plan New strict government savings → Czechia successfully avoided punishment from Europe (EU rules)

Public Debt Path

📊 What Happened

  • State debt jumped wildly in 2020 to fight the Covid lockdowns
  • The debt kept growing heavily through 2021 and 2022
  • For 2024–2026, the debt is expected to grow very slowly because the government is saving money and the economy is growing again

📐 Key Numbers

  • The country's debt is currently around 45% of its total economy (GDP)
  • This is still very safe and well below the breaking points set by Czech law and EU law
  • The danger "brake" level of 55% might accidentally be hit in 5 to 7 years
  • But right now: blaming the huge inflation strictly on the FTPL (government debt) seems very unlikely because 45% is not dangerously high.
💡 European view: Politicians and journalists inside Czechia think their finances are terrible, but international bankers completely disagree. Compared to the rest of the EU, Czechia is doing quite well. It completely obeys both European safety rules (deficit < 3%, debt < 60%). The government has less free money than before, but it is not out of money.

History Lesson: The Euro Debt Crisis

The European crisis in 2010–2012 is the most obvious modern proof of panic-driven fiscal dominance. The cost to borrow money (yields) for 10 years exploded violently for weak countries:

Country Highest Cost (Yield) Normal Cost (2006) What Happened
🇬🇷 Greece ~30% ~4% Massive hidden debt, bankruptcy, needed to be rescued
🇵🇹 Portugal ~15% ~4% No growth, terrible debt, forced to beg Europe for money
🇮🇪 Ireland ~14% ~4% Bank collapse → destroyed the government, needed rescue
🇮🇹 Italy ~7% ~4% Very old high debt, zero growth, panic spread from Greece
🇪🇸 Spain ~7% ~4% Housing crash, banking panic
🇩🇪 Germany ~1.5% ~4% Investors got scared and hid their money in safe Germany → rates actually dropped

The European rule for borrowing costs (which you need to join the Euro) sets a strict safe limit. During the crisis, multiple countries smashed right through this limit — proving how incredibly fast a tiny fear about government debt can explode into a total country crisis.

Fiscal-Monetary Nexus in Real Life: The Czech Case

So how do the government (fiscal) and the central bank (monetary) actually talk to each other in Czechia? Their legal rules are specifically built to stop fiscal dominance from ever happening:

  1. A very powerful, independent Central Bank

    The Czech National Bank (CNB) answers to nobody. It is strictly illegal for politicians to give them orders, and it is entirely illegal for the bank to print money for the state. The Minister of Finance is allowed to sit in their meetings but has absolutely no power to vote.

  2. No secret teamwork

    There is zero official teamwork between the bank's money plans and the government's spending plans. The government simply spends what it wants. The central bank watches this spending and treats it like outside weather — it adjusts its own plans to survive the weather, but it never obeys the government.

  3. Government action is just a guessing tool

    When the central bank builds its big computer predictions for the economy, it just copies the government's tax plans as a piece of data. The bank predicts what will happen, but it never tries to tell the government what to do.

  4. No free money given to the state

    Historically, the central bank has transferred zero printed money or profits to the government — proving perfectly that they refuse to secretly fund the state.

How Government Spending Changes the Bank's Predictions

The picture above explains exactly how the central bank believes government spending changes the country's growth:

Step 1

Government Choices

The decisions made by politicians: raising taxes, building roads, giving money to the poor, or handing out business support.

Step 2

The Money Shock

How those choices shake the economy: how much the state buys, how much cash families have left over, how much people shop, how businesses invest, and how prices move.

Step 3

Growth Impact

All these waves ultimately change the speed of the country's GDP growth. The bank studies this in two ways: the big picture (overall speed difference) and the detailed picture (exactly which part of the spending caused the change).

Detail How the Bank Handles It

Applying the Lesson: The Czech Republic is a perfect real-life example of the Monetary Active / Fiscal Passive setup. The Central Bank operates completely alone to crush inflation, treats government spending simply as outside weather, and absolutely refuses to print money for state debts (obeying Article 123 of European law). So, is there fiscal dominance in Czechia? The answer is a hard no. Building debt (45% of GDP) is still incredibly safe compared to crisis levels, and the Central Bank proved its total independence during the huge post-Covid inflation wave. However, long-term safety is not guaranteed: while EU rules act like strong seatbelts today, an aging population of grandpas and expensive military needs will eventually crush the budget if politicians don't reform the laws soon.

Czechia is not suffering from fiscal dominance. The central bank is incredibly powerful and independent, money printing for the state is banned, and national debt is safely lower than European limits. However, the future is not completely secure unless politicians modernize the budget. The FTPL theory blaming inflation entirely on state debt is simply wrong for Czechia because a 45% debt level is completely manageable.
