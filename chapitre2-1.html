2.1 — Why Study Inequality & Theory

Chapter 2: Inequality

Why Study Inequality?

Inequality — the unfair sharing of income, wealth, or life chances among people in a society — is one of the main topics in public finance. But why should economists and leaders care about it? There are two very different kinds of reasons, and it's important to know the difference between them.

Motivation 1

🔧 Instrumental Reasons (The Effects)

Inequality matters because of its results. Even if you don't care about inequality itself, you might care about the problems it creates. Stiglitz (2012) explains that high inequality has real, bad effects on society:

  • Less social trust — when the gap between the rich and poor gets bigger, trust between groups drops, politics becomes angry, and the feeling of community gets weaker.
  • More crime — places with higher inequality usually have more crime, because extreme poverty makes people desperate and lowers social spending.
  • Worse health — very unequal societies usually have worse overall health, even when average money is the same. The stress of being poor changes physical and mental health.

In short: even a selfish society should care about inequality, because inequality hurts everyone through these problems.

Motivation 2

⚖️ Intrinsic Motivation (The Values)

Inequality matters in itself, based on a general idea of justice or social well-being. This is a normative idea: we believe that sharing things more equally is morally better, no matter the real-world effects.

The classic argument comes from utilitarianism: if we accept that the value of an extra euro goes down (an extra €100 helps someone earning €500/month much more than someone earning €50,000/month), then too much inequality lowers the total happiness in society. Taking from the rich and giving to the poor increases total well-being.

This difference was shown by Atkinson (2015): instrumental reasons talk about effects; intrinsic reasons talk about values.

🎯 Key difference (Atkinson, 2015): You should be able to argue against inequality from both sides on a test. The instrumental argument uses real facts (inequality causes X, Y, Z); the intrinsic argument uses morals and welfare theories (inequality is unfair because...).

The Kuznets Curve

Theory — Kuznets (1955)

The Kuznets Curve is the idea that building factories and economic growth lead first to a rise and then to a drop in inequality. The link between average income and inequality looks like an upside-down U shape (∩).

How does this upside-down U shape happen? It takes three steps:

  1. Phase 1 — Low development, low inequality

    In early economies, most people work in the same area (usually farming). Incomes are low for everyone but also very similar. There is little inequality because there are small differences — everyone is poor together.

  2. Phase 2 — Factories open, rising inequality

    As the economy starts building factories, new and better jobs appear. But only a small group of workers get this new money first — those who go to the factories, those who own businesses, or those in cities. The rest stay in farming or older jobs with less money. This gap makes inequality jump up. Factory workers earn more than farmers; trained workers earn more than untrained ones; some cities grow faster than others.

  3. Phase 3 — Advanced development, falling inequality

    Later, more and more workers move to modern jobs, making better money. Schools become normal for everyone, people move up in life, and governments create sharing rules (taxes for the rich, social help, public schools). These things shrink the money gap, and inequality drops. The point $y^*$ on the graph shows the money level where inequality hits the top before going down.

Critical Detail: The Kuznets Curve is an idea, not a perfect economic law. It showed what happened to early rich countries (UK, USA in the 19th–20th century), but modern facts show a harder story. Milanovic (2016) shows that some rich countries have seen inequality go up again after years of dropping — creating a second "wave." Economic growth does not naturally lower inequality; it depends on rules, sharing policies, and technology changes.

Kuznets Curve Revisited — UK & USA

When we look at the real history for the UK and USA over hundreds of years, the story is more confusing than the simple upside-down U. Instead of a clean ∩ shape, we find something holding an N-shape or even an S-shape, with three different periods:

  1. Rising inequality during factory growth (pre-1940)

    In both the UK (around 1867–1913, the top of the Industrial Revolution) and the USA (1860–1929), inequality went up fast. A small group of factory owners, business founders, and investors became extremely rich, while city workers and farm people got much smaller rewards.

  2. Falling inequality, middle of the 20th century

    Between roughly 1913–1978 (UK) and 1933–1979 (USA), inequality dropped a lot. This happened for several strong reasons: high taxes for the rich, the creation of the welfare state (social help), schools for everyone, strong worker unions, and the loss of huge family fortunes during two world wars and the Great Depression.

  3. Rising inequality again, since the 1980s

    Starting around 1980, inequality started growing again in both countries — very fast in the USA, a bit slower in the UK. The reasons are: globalization (sending jobs to cheaper countries), technology helping smart workers (computers helping highly educated people), lower taxes for the rich (top tax rates fell), financial growth (money making money faster than work does), and weaker unions.

🚨 Critical point: The Kuznets thought — that growth alone will one day stop inequality — is not supported by today's data. Economic growth does not automatically make people more equal. Rules, laws, and huge economic changes matter the most. A country can get richer and more unequal at the same time.

Axioms of Inequality Measurement

Before we can test inequality, we must agree on the rules a good inequality tool (measure) should follow. Economists have found several main axioms — logical rules that any good formula for inequality should obey. Knowing these rules is very important because they tell us which tools we can trust and how to read them.

Axiom 1

Transfer Principle (Pigou-Dalton Principle)

Axiom 1

Transfer Principle: If we move a sum of money from person A to a poorer person B — without changing their ranking in society — then inequality must go down. The opposite is true: taking from the poor and giving to the rich must increase inequality.

This is the simplest rule to understand. Look at the two examples above: $A = (0, 10, 10, 10, 10)$ and $B = (3, 7, 10, 10, 10)$. Both groups have the same total money (40), but in group B, 3 coins are moved from the second person (10→7) to the first person (0→3). The poorest person is less poor now, and nobody changed their ranking place. By the Transfer Principle:

$A(0, 10, 10, 10, 10) \succ_I B(3, 7, 10, 10, 10)$

This math formula means "A has more inequality than B." The transfer from an richer person to a poorer one — without changing who is richer — reduced inequality. Any good inequality formula must show this.

Axiom 2

Anonymity (Symmetry)

Axiom 2

Anonymity Principle: Changing who has what money does not change the inequality score. Everyone's position matters equally . This means the score of inequality only depends on the list of incomes in society, not on which specific person has which income.

The picture shows three groups that share the exact same incomes: $(3, 7, 10, 10, 10)$. The only difference is which person gets which money. If two people trade their incomes, the big picture is exactly the same — and so our inequality score must remain identical.

$A(3, 7, 10, 10, 10) \sim_I B(10, 10, 3, 7, 10) \sim_I C(7, 10, 10, 10, 3)$

This rule is valuable because inequality tests must measure the distance of incomes across society, not who the people are. If person 1 or person 5 is very poor, it does not change the total problem of inequality — what matters is that someone is poor.

Axiom 3

Scale Invariance

Axiom 3

Scale Invariance: Multiplying all incomes by the exact same number does not change the inequality score. If everyone gets twice as rich (or twice as poor), the inequality remains exactly the same.

The groups above — $A = (0, 10, 10, 10, 10)$ and $B = (0, 20, 20, 20, 20)$ — share the exact same relative shape. Every money amount in B is exactly two times the amount in A. The percentage differences between people haven't changed: the poorest person still has 0%, and the rest share equally.

$A(0, 10, 10, 10, 10) \sim_I B(0, 20, 20, 20, 20)$

This rule shows that inequality measures are about relative percentage differences, not raw money amounts. A country where everyone makes double is a richer country, but not a more or less equal country. This is why tools like the Gini coefficient are shown as percentages or ratios (from 0 to 1) — they only measure relative inequality.

Why This Matters: A very important result of scale invariance: if all salaries in a country double because of inflation (and prices double too), the Gini coefficient will not change. This means inequality tools measure percentage differences in the society, not real buying power. It also means you cannot compare Gini coefficients between countries to see who has better living standards — it only shows how fairly the money is shared.

Axiom 4

Decomposability

Axiom 4

Decomposability: Total inequality can be written as the addition of inequality between groups plus inequality inside groups.

This rule is incredibly helpful for checking policies. Take a country with incomes $A = (0, 10, 10, 10, 10)$. We can break this into two smaller groups: $B = (0, 10)$ and $C = (10, 10, 10)$. Total inequality in A can now be separated into:

Between-Group Inequality

The inequality that comes from differences between the group averages. Group B has an average of 5, Group C has an average of 10 — this gap adds to total inequality.

Within-Group Inequality

The inequality inside each specific group. Inside Group B, incomes jump from 0 to 10 (very high inequality). Inside Group C, all incomes are 10 (zero inequality).

Why is this useful? Because it helps leaders spot where inequality actually starts. For example: does the inequality in France come mostly from differences between large regions (between-group), or from differences found inside the same city (within-group)? Knowing the answer tells us which policies will fix the problem best.

The four rules above are the most popular, but there are more:

  • Translation invariance: Giving the same flat amount to all incomes does not change inequality. (Be careful: this completely fights scale invariance — you cannot have both rules at the same time, so tools must pick one or the other.)
  • Population principle: If we copy the population (every single person is cloned with their exact income), the inequality score remains identical.
