8.3 — Translating Votes into Mandates

Chapter 8: Public Choice Theory — The Mathematics of Seat Allocation

Problem 2b: From Votes to Seats

Once citizens have voted, we face a deeply practical problem: how do we convert millions of votes into a small number of parliamentary seats? A parliament might have 200 seats, but millions of votes were cast across dozens of parties. There is no obvious, "natural" way to do this conversion — it requires a mathematical formula, and different formulas produce very different parliaments.

This is largely a mathematical issue, but don't underestimate its importance. The choice of seat allocation method can determine which party forms the government, which coalition is possible, and ultimately which policies affect millions of lives.

There are two main families of decisions that must be made:

🗺️ Constituency Design

  • What is the level? One national constituency (like the Netherlands) or many regional ones (like Czechia with 14 regions)?
  • How many mandates per constituency? More seats per constituency = more proportional results.
  • What to do with remainders? This is where the real math happens (see below).

🏛️ Political Party Rules

  • Closed vs. open list? Closed list = the party decides the order of candidates. Open list = voters can choose which specific candidates from the party they prefer.
  • Threshold to enter? E.g. in Czechia, a party must get at least 5% of all votes to receive any seats. This prevents extreme fragmentation.

Two Families of Seat Allocation Methods

The critical challenge is always dealing with remainders. For example, if a party wins 33.3% of votes but there are only 10 seats, they "deserve" exactly 3.33 seats — but you can't give someone a third of a seat! How you handle that 0.33 remainder changes everything. There are two families of methods:

Family 1

📐 Highest Averages Methods

Seats are assigned one at a time using a quotient formula. After each round, the winning party's quotient drops, making it harder for them to win the next seat. This creates a self-balancing mechanism.

Members: D'Hondt, Sainte-Laguë, Imperiali, Huntington-Hill.

Family 2

📊 Largest Remainder Methods

A fixed quota (election number) is calculated. Votes are divided by this quota. The integer parts give automatic seats. The remaining seats go to parties with the highest leftover fractions.

Members: Hamilton method with Hare quota, Hamilton method with Droop quota.

The D'Hondt Method (Jefferson Method)

The D'Hondt method is the most widely used seat allocation method in the world. It is used in Belgium, the Netherlands, Finland, Spain, Portugal, Argentina, and many other countries. Here is exactly how it works:

The D'Hondt Formula

After each party receives its votes $V$, mandates are assigned one at a time in successive rounds. In each round, we calculate a quotient for every party:

$Q = \dfrac{V}{m + 1}$

Where $V$ is the number of votes received by the party, and $m$ is the number of mandates already assigned to that party (initially $m = 0$ for all parties). The party with the highest quotient wins the seat in that round.

The beauty of this system is that every time a party wins a seat, its denominator increases by 1, making its quotient smaller. This means large parties gradually lose their advantage as they accumulate seats, giving smaller parties a chance.

Let's work through a complete example step by step:

Setup: 4 mandates must be allocated. 3 parties competed:

  • Party A: 100 votes (44.4%)
  • Party B: 80 votes (35.6%)
  • Party C: 45 votes (20.0%)
Round Party A (100 votes) Party B (80 votes) Party C (45 votes) Winner
Round 1 $\frac{100}{0+1} = 100$ ✅ $\frac{80}{0+1} = 80$ $\frac{45}{0+1} = 45$ Party A
Round 2 $\frac{100}{1+1} = 50$ $\frac{80}{0+1} = 80$ ✅ $\frac{45}{0+1} = 45$ Party B
Round 3 $\frac{100}{1+1} = 50$ ✅ $\frac{80}{1+1} = 40$ $\frac{45}{0+1} = 45$ Party A
Round 4 $\frac{100}{2+1} = 33.3$ $\frac{80}{1+1} = 40$ $\frac{45}{0+1} = 45$ ✅ Party C

📊 Final Result

Party A: 2 seats (50%)
Party B: 1 seat (25%)
Party C: 1 seat (25%)

⚖️ Proportionality Check

Votes: 44.4% / 35.6% / 20.0%
Seats: 50.0% / 25.0% / 25.0%

Notice that D'Hondt slightly favors the largest party (Party A gets 50% of seats with only 44.4% of votes) at the expense of Party B.

⚠️ Key Bias: The D'Hondt method systematically favors larger parties. This is because the quotient formula uses $m + 1$ in the denominator, which drops more slowly for big parties. If strict proportionality is your goal, D'Hondt is not the best choice — but it does produce more stable parliaments with fewer tiny parties.

Other Highest-Average Methods

The D'Hondt method is just one member of the "highest averages" family. The other members use the same mechanism but different quotient formulas, which change the bias toward large or small parties:

Method Quotient Formula Divisor Sequence Bias
D'Hondt $Q = \frac{V}{m + 1}$ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ... Favors large parties
Sainte-Laguë $Q = \frac{V}{2m + 1}$ 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, ... More proportional (fairer to small parties)
Imperiali $Q = \frac{V}{m + \text{seq}}$ 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, ... Strongly favors large parties
Huntington-Hill $Q = \frac{V}{\sqrt{n(n+1)}}$ Uses geometric mean Balanced (used for US Congress)

The key insight here is that the divisor sequence determines everything. If the divisors grow slowly (D'Hondt: 1, 2, 3...), the quotients stay high for large parties, meaning they keep winning seats. If the divisors grow fast (Sainte-Laguë: 1, 3, 5...), the quotients drop much more quickly, giving smaller parties a better chance.

For the exam: You must understand the D'Hondt method well enough to calculate a full example by hand. Know the formula $Q = V/(m+1)$, work through the rounds, and explain why the method slightly favors larger parties. Knowing that Sainte-Laguë uses $2m+1$ and is more proportional earns extra credit.

The Hamilton Method (Largest Remainder Method)

The second family of methods is completely different in approach. Instead of assigning seats one by one through rounds, the Hamilton method uses a fixed quota to divide all votes at once.

The Hamilton Method — Step by Step
  1. Calculate the quota: Choose either the Hare quota or the Droop quota.
  2. Divide each party's votes by the quota. This gives a number with an integer part and a remainder (decimal part).
  3. The integer part gives each party its automatic seats.
  4. The remaining unallocated seats are distributed one by one to the parties with the highest remainders, until all seats are filled.

Hare Quota

$Q_{\text{Hare}} = \dfrac{\text{Total Votes}}{\text{Total Seats}}$

With 100,000 votes and 10 seats: $Q = 10,000$. You need 10,000 votes to earn one seat automatically.

Droop Quota (Hagenbach-Bischoff)

$Q_{\text{Droop}} = \dfrac{\text{Total Votes}}{\text{Total Seats} + 1}$

With 100,000 votes and 10 seats: $Q = 9,091$. The lower quota makes it slightly easier to earn automatic seats.

Complete Worked Example — Hamilton Method with Hare Quota:

Party Yellows Whites Reds Greens Blues Pinks Total
Votes 47,000 16,000 15,800 12,000 6,100 3,100 100,000
Votes / Quota 4.70 1.60 1.58 1.20 0.61 0.31 —
Automatic Seats 4 1 1 1 0 0 7
Remainder 0.70 0.60 0.58 0.20 0.61 0.31 —
Bonus Seats +1 +1 0 0 +1 0 3
TOTAL SEATS 5 2 1 1 1 0 10

Notice something interesting: the Blues party (6,100 votes = 6.1%) received 1 seat (10%), while the Pinks party (3,100 votes = 3.1%) received zero. The Blues beat the Reds in the remainder race (0.61 > 0.58) despite the Reds having far more total votes (15,800 vs. 6,100). This shows that the remainder lottery can produce surprising results.

🔑 Hamilton vs. D'Hondt: The Hamilton method tends to be more favorable to smaller parties than D'Hondt, because the remainder allocation gives small parties a fighting chance. D'Hondt's round-by-round process systematically advantages parties with the most votes.

Voting in Practice: The Czech System

The Czech Republic has developed a complex voting system that combines elements from several methods. Understanding this system is essential because it shows how theoretical methods are combined and modified to serve real political goals.

Czech Chamber of Deputies — Electoral Rules
  • System type: Proportional representation by political party, organized by region.
  • Entry threshold: A party needs at least 5% of all national votes to enter parliament. Without this threshold, parliament would be fragmented into dozens of tiny parties, making stable government impossible.
  • Coalition thresholds: If two parties form a coalition, they need 8%. Three or more parties in coalition need 11%. This prevents parties from "gaming" the system by merging just to pass the 5% barrier.
  • Mandate distribution across regions: Uses the Hare quota to distribute mandates to different constituencies.
  • Mandate distribution to parties (since 2021): Uses the Imperiali quota — one of the methods that most strongly favors large parties. This was a controversial reform that significantly benefited the ruling coalition at the time.
⚠️ Real-World Implication: The 2021 shift from D'Hondt to Imperiali was politically motivated — it helped the governing parties win more seats with fewer votes. This is a perfect example of how the mathematical choice of seat allocation is never politically neutral. The people in power always have an incentive to choose the formula that benefits them most.

Quick Comparison of Real Electoral Systems:

  • Czech Republic (CZ): Proportional representation. Multi-party parliament with coalition governments. Parties need 5% to enter. Uses Imperiali/Hare quotas.
  • United States: First-past-the-post ("winner takes all") in each district. Produces a two-party system because third parties can never win enough districts. The Electoral College system means a president can win without the popular vote (as in 2000 and 2016).
  • France: Two-round system for president and parliament. First round with all candidates; second round between top two (president) or candidates above 12.5% (parliament).
  • Slovakia: Single nationwide constituency with proportional representation. 5% threshold. Very proportional results but often leads to highly fragmented parliaments.

Chapter 8.3 — The Big Picture:

  • Translating votes into seats requires choosing constituency design and a mathematical method. Different methods produce different parliaments.
  • Two families: Highest Averages (D'Hondt, Sainte-Laguë, Imperiali) vs. Largest Remainder (Hamilton with Hare/Droop).
  • D'Hondt: $Q = V/(m+1)$. Assigns seats round by round. Slightly favors large parties.
  • Sainte-Laguë: $Q = V/(2m+1)$. More proportional, fairer to small parties.
  • Hamilton/Hare: Quota = Total Votes / Seats. Integer parts give automatic seats; highest remainders get the rest. Also relatively fair to small parties.
  • Czech system: Proportional representation, 5% threshold, Hare for regions, Imperiali since 2021 for parties (strongly favoring large parties).
  • Bottom line: The choice of formula is NEVER neutral — it always benefits some parties at the expense of others. This is a deeply political decision disguised as a mathematical one.
