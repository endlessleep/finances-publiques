5.3 — Fiscal Federalism & The Innovation Gap

Chapter 5: Central and Local Government Public Finance

Fiscal Federalism: Central vs. Local

Not all public finance happens at the national level. Fiscal Federalism explores the optimal allocation of responsibilities and powers between different levels of government.

Main Aspects of Fiscal Federalism: The relationship between central and local government is defined by three structural imbalances that must be managed:

  • Fiscal Capacity: The ability of a region to raise tax revenue varies enormously — a wealthy capital like Prague or Paris generates far more tax revenue per capita than a rural village. This uneven capacity is one of the strongest arguments for vertical redistribution (transfers from the central government to poorer regions).
  • Vertical Imbalance: This is the structural mismatch between who collects the taxes (usually the central government) and who delivers the services (usually sub-central governments — regions, municipalities, cities). If regions are responsible for running schools and hospitals but cannot raise enough revenue locally, they depend entirely on central transfers.
  • Horizontal Imbalance: Even at the same level of government, some regions have higher needs (e.g., more elderly residents requiring healthcare) but lower funding capacity (e.g., depopulated areas with a shrinking tax base). This creates a double disadvantage that only inter-regional transfers can fix.
🔗 The Wicksellian Connection: A healthy democracy requires a link between those who pay the taxes, those who raise them, and those who benefit. Local autonomy strengthens this link.

Regional Redistribution Principles

When the state moves money from "rich" regions to "poor" regions, it follows specific technical objectives:

1. Revenue Equalisation

The central government ensures that every region — regardless of its own tax base — can provide a minimum level of public services. Without this, citizens in poor regions would receive drastically worse education, healthcare, and infrastructure than those in wealthy urban centres.

2. Cost Equalisation

Even with equal revenue, delivering the same service can cost much more in some regions than others. Mountainous terrain, remote island geography, or extreme weather conditions make infrastructure and service delivery significantly more expensive. Cost equalisation compensates for these structural disadvantages.

3. Regional Convergence

Going beyond mere equalisation, this principle uses budget transfers to close the economic gap between lagging regions and centres of wealth. The EU's Cohesion Policy and Structural Funds are the most prominent example — they channel investment into infrastructure, education, and business development in less-developed member states.

Empirical Evidence: The Innovation & R&D Gap

In the global race for future growth, R&D Intensity (R&D as % of GDP) is the decisive factor. Current data shows a structural divergence between world leaders.

Comparison of Models (Draghi Report 2024):

  • United States: The undisputed leader. Driven by the private sector and a dynamic ecosystem of venture capital and decentralized academic-industrial links.
  • China: Catching up at a staggering speed. Utilizing a hybrid model where the state provides massive strategic funding to "key" industries.
  • European Union: Lagging behind both. The EU depends more on public funding than the US, but faces a massive private incentive problem.
⚔️ The Fragmentation Trap: In the US, federal funding is centralized and coordinated. In the EU, funding is fragmented between 27 member states, preventing economies of scale and creating a "competitiveness deficit."

The State as an Innovator (Mazzucato)

Why should the state invest in R&D if the private sector is more efficient? Mariana Mazzucato argues that the state's role is not just to "fix" markets, but to create them.

The Mazzucato Thesis: Most radical technologies (GPS, The Internet, Touchscreens, Biotechnology) were funded by the state early on. The state acts as a Mission-Oriented risk-taker that explores "blue-sky" research that private firms are too risk-averse to touch.

The Draghi Priority (2024–2030): Mario Draghi's landmark report calls for a strategic transformation of how Europe funds and organises its economic future:

  • Closing the Innovation Gap: Europe must move from being a regulator of technology to being an active investor and creator of technology. This means shifting from defensive policies (antitrust, GDPR) to offensive ones (public venture capital, strategic R&D programmes).
  • Decarbonization + Competitiveness: Rather than treating the green transition as a cost, Europe should use it as an industrial opportunity — investing in clean energy, battery manufacturing, and green hydrogen to create new industries and jobs.
  • Strategic Resilience: The pandemic and the war in Ukraine exposed Europe's dangerous dependencies on foreign energy, semiconductors, and critical minerals. Draghi argues for reducing these dependencies through coordinated investment in domestic supply chains and defence capability.

Defence: The Return of Geopolitics

After the Cold War, most advanced economies experienced a "Peace Dividend" — a massive decline in defense spending as % of GDP. That era has ended.

Current Trends: While the modern state remains a "Welfare State" (where most public spending goes to social transfers like pensions and healthcare), defence is rising back up in total spending. Russia's dramatic military budget increase and the ongoing war in Ukraine are forcing European governments to rethink their fiscal priorities. The United States maintains a stable, high level of defence spending (~3.5% GDP), while most EU members have struggled to meet even the NATO target of 2% of GDP.

The core tension is clear: how can Europe simultaneously increase defence spending, fund the green transition, sustain its welfare state, and reduce debt? This is the ultimate test of the Triple Bind (Barber, 2015) introduced in Chapter 5.1.

🏮 Core Conclusion: Empirical evidence shows that while government intervention is essential to correct market failures such as externalities, differences in institutional structure, incentives, and coordination lead to significant variation in economic performance across regions.
💡

Chapter 5 Key Takeaways

  • Fiscal Federalism is about finding the right balance between centralisation (better coordination, economies of scale) and decentralisation (closer to citizens, better accountability through the Wicksellian connection).
  • The Innovation Gap is Europe's biggest structural weakness: fragmented R&D funding across 27 member states prevents the economies of scale that the US and China achieve.
  • Draghi's Call (2024): Europe needs joint investment in innovation, green industry, and defence — not just regulation. The era of relying on the US security umbrella and cheap Russian energy is definitively over.
  • The return of geopolitical risk means that defence spending is no longer optional — it is a fiscal priority that directly competes with the welfare state for limited resources.
