2.2 — Measuring Inequality

Chapter 2: Inequality

Inequality Indices

Once we agree on the rules a good inequality measure should follow, we can build specific indices (math formulas). Each formula measures different parts of how money is spread out. Here are the main types:

Decile Ratios

The easiest way to measure inequality is to compare specific points in society. Decile ratios take the money earned by one group and divide it by the money earned by another:

Ratio What It Compares What It Captures
90/10 Income at the 90th percentile ÷ Income at the 10th percentile Total distance: how much more the rich make compared to the poor.
90/50 Income at the 90th percentile ÷ Median (middle) income Top-half gap: how far the rich are from normal, middle-class people.
80/20 Income at the 80th percentile ÷ Income at the 20th percentile A softer version of 90/10, dropping the very richest and very poorest people.

Decile ratios are simple to understand and explain (for example, "the top earner makes 9 times what the bottom earner makes"), but they only look at two points in the society and ignore everyone else in between.

The Gini Coefficient

Formula

The Gini coefficient is the most famous inequality measure. It compares every single pair of incomes in the country:

$G = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} |y_i - y_j|}{2n^2 \bar{y}}$

The top part adds up the exact differences between every pair of people $(i, j)$. The bottom part divides this by the number of pairs ($n^2$) and the average income ($\bar{y}$). This gives a number between 0 (perfect equality, everyone is the same) and 1 (maximum inequality, one person has everything).

In simple words, the Gini coefficient asks: "If I randomly pick two people, how different are their incomes, compared to the normal average?" A Gini score of 0.30 means that the average difference between two random people is 60% of the average income (because the formula divides it by $2\bar{y}$).

Other Indices

Atkinson Index

Includes a special inequality aversion number $\varepsilon$. The higher this number is, the more the formula cares about helping the poorest people. This makes it react more strongly to poverty than the Gini, and it connects directly to social welfare ideas.

Generalized Entropy (GE) Measures

A family of formulas that includes the Theil index (GE(1)) and the Mean Log Deviation (GE(0)). Their biggest superpower is that they can be perfectly broken into pieces: total inequality can be split exactly into between-group and within-group problems (Axiom 4).

The Lorenz Curve

The Lorenz curve is the best way to draw a picture of income inequality in a country. It works by putting two things on a graph:

  • X-axis (bottom line): Percentage of the population, stacked from poorest to richest (0% to 100%).
  • Y-axis (side line): Percentage of the total money owned by that group of people.

Two lines appear on this graph:

Line of Perfect Equality (Straight Line)

If money was perfectly shared, the bottom 10% of people would own exactly 10% of the money, the bottom 50% would own 50%, and so on. This makes a straight 45° diagonal line. It acts as a perfect goal — the closer the curved line is to this straight line, the more equal the country is.

Lorenz Curve (Blue Curve)

The real distribution always curves under the straight line (unless there is perfect equality). When the curve drops far below the straight line, it means the poorest people hold a tiny amount of total money. For example, if the blue curve hits the point (60, 30), it means the poorest 60% of the population holds only 30% of the total money.

Link to Gini

The Gini coefficient comes directly from this Lorenz curve graph. It is equal to the space between the straight line and the curved line, divided by the total space under the straight line:

$\text{Gini} = \frac{\text{Area between diagonal and Lorenz curve}}{\text{Total area below diagonal}} = \frac{A}{A + B}$

If the blue curve perfectly covers the straight line (perfect sharing), the space is 0 → Gini = 0. If the curve touches the very bottom and right edges (one person has everything), the space is as big as possible → Gini becomes 1.

Measuring Income

Income seems like a simple idea, but measuring it properly is one of the hardest jobs in real public finance. The choices we pick for how to measure money can completely change our final answer about inequality.

Why do we measure income? Because it is the clearest sign of whether someone has enough resources — it shows buying power, life safety, and living standards.

Challenges (The Problems):

  • Honesty of reporting — People often say they have less money than they actually do in surveys (especially very rich people and those making secret money).
  • Family vs. single person money — Should we measure the money of one person or a whole house? Families share money, but maybe they don't share it fairly.
  • Short-term vs. long life money — A college student might have very little money this year but will make huge money later. Looking at only one year can be wrong.
  • Price differences — €1,000 every month buys completely different things in Paris compared to a small town. We must fix numbers for buying power.
  • Different types of money — Which kind of income we measure changes everything. This course looks at four kinds:
Concept What It Includes Formula
Market Income Work salary + investing money + private gifts What you make from the economy, before the government steps in at all.
Gross Income Market income + government help (pensions, jobless money, social aid) Market income + state transfers.
Disposable Income Gross income − direct taxes (income tax, social fees) What you actually have in your pocket to spend.
Extended Income Disposable income + value of free public goods (schools, hospitals, roads) The most complete picture of a person's life quality.
💡 Key insight: Inequality is almost always highest when looking at Market Income and lowest for Extended Income. The difference between them shows the redistributive impact of the government — proving how much taxes and social help make the country more equal.

An extra trick is equivalization: this means dividing the house's total spending money by the number of "equivalent adults" to count the family size properly. A family of 4 with €4,000/month is not as rich as a single guy with €4,000/month, because costs are shared but not strictly multiplied by 4 (a bigger house is more expensive, but not 4 times more expensive).

Data Challenges: Surveys vs. Official Records

Survey Problems

Surveys asking about money use two groups. The normal random sample (asking normal people everywhere) gets around 65-70% of people to answer. But the rich list sample — targeting wealthy families — gets only 30-35% answers. The richest people are the hardest to question, meaning standard surveys always fail to count the real wealth at the very top.

Official Records vs. Surveys

When measuring how much government help kills poverty, administrative data (official tax and state records) always shows a bigger success than survey data (asking people). This difference is huge for weak groups like single mothers and sick people, because they often forget or don't know the exact name of the government help they got.

The Data Quality Problem: Every inequality number has hidden rules. The source matters deeply: surveys miss the super-rich (they hide their money), while official tax data misses secret cash jobs. The income type matters: market income (before tax) paints a completely different story from disposable income (after tax). And time matters: a young lawyer making €30k looks "poor" today, but will be rich eventually. Good answers in an exam always explain these hidden choices clearly.

Beyond Income: Wealth, Shopping & Opportunities

Dimension 2

💎 Wealth

Why measure it? Wealth (what you own minus what you owe) is much more unequal than income. It shows deep long-term power, life safety, and huge advantages given to children.

Challenges: Wealth is terribly hard to track. The super rich hide their money using tricky tax laws and complex bank tricks. Choosing what counts (pensions? houses?) and guessing the price of things that aren't for sale are big problems.

Dimension 3

🛒 Consumption (Spending)

Why measure it? How much people buy and use can show living standards better than income, because people balance their money over time (borrowing when young, saving for later). It shows if someone actually enjoys a good life.

Challenges: Using things vs. spending on things (living in your own paid house is useful, but costs no extra money), differences in quality, bad survey answers, and mixing family items with personal items.

Dimension 4

🎯 Opportunities

Why measure it? Many smart speakers say we must focus on equal chances instead of just equal results. The main question: does your money future depend on your hard work and smarts, or entirely on how rich your parents are?

Challenges: Measuring "chances" needs data that lasts a very long time (watching the exact same people for 30 years). We can try to measure inequality by looking at who gets degrees, who stays healthy, or combining multiple scores.
