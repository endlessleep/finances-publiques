Chapter 7: Health Economics — Theory, Systems, and the Cost of Aging

Four Healthcare System Models

Every country's healthcare system can be classified into one of four broad models, each with distinct financing and provision mechanisms. In reality, most countries are hybrids — but understanding the pure types is essential for comparison.

Model Financing Provision Examples
Beveridge Tax-funded Government-owned UK (NHS), Spain, Nordic countries
Bismarck Social insurance Mix public/private Germany, France, Japan, Czech Republic
National Health Insurance Tax-funded Private providers Canada, Taiwan, South Korea

US (hybrid) Mix of all models Mix of all models Medicare, Medicaid, employer-sponsored, uninsured

🇬🇧 Beveridge (1942)

Named after William Beveridge, who designed the UK's NHS. The government both finances and provides healthcare, funded from general taxation. Citizens pay nothing at the point of use (or very small co-payments). The government controls costs through global budgets and waiting lists. Strengths: universal access, low administrative costs, strong cost control. Weaknesses: rationing by waiting, less patient choice.

🇩🇪 Bismarck (1883)

Named after Otto von Bismarck, who created Germany's social insurance system. Workers and employers pay into non-profit insurance funds ("sickness funds"), which then purchase care from a mix of public and private providers. The system is employment-based but regulated to ensure universal coverage. Strengths: high patient satisfaction (91% in Switzerland), provider choice. Weaknesses: higher administrative costs, equity concerns for non-employed.

Comparing System Performance (2025 Data)

How do these models compare in practice? The evidence is mixed and context-dependent:

✅ Beveridge Advantages

Beveridge systems achieve better cost control and lower administrative overhead. Single-payer financing eliminates the duplicated billing and marketing costs of multi-insurer systems. Total administrative costs in the UK NHS are roughly 2% of spending, versus 15–20% in the US.

✅ Bismarck Advantages

Bismarck systems score higher on patient satisfaction — Switzerland reaches 91%. Patients have more choice of provider and typically shorter waiting times for elective procedures. The employment-based financing also creates a clear link between contributions and entitlement.

📊 A 2025 study across 38 OECD countries found no evidence that public → private transition improves efficiency. Health outcomes show slight advantages for Beveridge systems, but these are not consistently statistically significant.

Expenditures Rising Fast

Health spending as a share of GDP has risen in virtually every country between 2000 and 2024. Increases typically range from 2 to 5 percentage points of GDP. Countries like the US, South Korea, and the UK are at the top of the distribution with particularly large increases, while very few countries show stagnation or slight decline.

📈 Key Fact: The health share of GDP increases everywhere — creating structural budgetary pressure. Unlike pensions, education, or long-term care (where some countries see stabilisation or decline), health spending is the only major expenditure category that rises in every single country.

Looking to the future, projections of age-related spending to 2070 confirm that this trajectory will continue. The main drivers are:

  • Pensions — the principal driver of total age-related spending growth.
  • Healthcare — systematically positive increases in every country without exception. Remember the Grossman model: as the population ages ($\delta_t$ rises), maintaining individual health becomes more expensive.
  • Long-term care — also rising strongly, driven by the expansion of the 80+ cohort.
  • Education and unemployment — sometimes stable or even negative, partially offsetting other increases.

Government Role in Healthcare Financing

Healthcare is financed through three main channels: direct government budgets (general taxation), social health insurance (compulsory contributions), and private spending (out-of-pocket payments and voluntary insurance). The mix varies enormously:

🇸🇪 Nordic Countries

Public financing dominant: 80–90% of total health spending is publicly funded through general taxation. Very low out-of-pocket costs. The state is the dominant payer, regulator, and often provider.

🇩🇪 Continental Europe

Financing is primarily through social insurance (employer + employee contributions). The government's role is mainly regulatory — setting the rules for the insurance funds and ensuring coverage. A higher proportion of private supplementary insurance compared to Beveridge systems.

🇺🇸 United States

The private share is very high compared to other rich countries. Healthcare is split between employer-sponsored insurance, Medicare (65+), Medicaid (low-income), and a significant uninsured population. This hybrid structure creates administrative complexity and cost inflation.

🌍 Emerging Economies

A mixed system with a strong private component. Out-of-pocket payments dominate in many low- and middle-income countries, creating catastrophic health expenditure risks for the poorest households.

Why Governments Intervene in Healthcare

Given the market failures identified by Arrow (1963) and the insurance failures described in Section 7.2, governments intervene in healthcare for several reasons:

  • Market failures: Information asymmetry, externalities (vaccination, disease surveillance), and public goods elements require government correction.
  • Adverse selection mandate: The government can impose a mandate for insurance and thus eliminate the adverse selection market failure that would otherwise cause the "death spiral" described in Section 7.2.
  • Merit goods: Society may decide that healthcare should be universal regardless of ability to pay — this is a normative judgement that goes beyond efficiency.
  • Redistribution: Health insurance acts as a mechanism for income and risk redistribution — the healthy subsidise the sick, and the wealthy subsidise the poor.
  • Human capital: A healthy workforce → higher productivity → GDP growth. Public health investment has external returns beyond the individual.
⚠️ However: Government provision creates its own problems. Moral hazard (over-provision, over-consumption) can be even more pronounced in government-run schemes, where the budget constraint is softer and political pressure favours expansion of services.

Efficiency: Does More Spending Buy Better Health?

One of the most striking findings in health economics is the weak relationship between spending and outcomes:

0.46

Correlation without US

Life expectancy across OECD countries in 2023 ranges from roughly 75 to 85 years. The correlation with per-capita health spending is surprisingly low — and drops dramatically when the US is included, because the US spends by far the most but achieves outcomes well below countries that spend half as much.

💡 Key Insight: More spending ≠ proportionally better health. What matters is the efficiency of the system — how well resources are allocated, not just how many resources are deployed. The US paradox illustrates this powerfully.

The Fundamental Problem — Scarcity

Healthcare needs are effectively unlimited, but resources are finite. Every health dollar spent has an opportunity cost — it could have been spent on education, infrastructure, or defence.

⚙️ Technical Efficiency

Maximise output from given inputs — minimise the cost per procedure. This is about doing things right: using the fewest resources to achieve a given health outcome.

📊 Allocative Efficiency

Direct resources to the interventions producing the greatest health gains. This is about doing the right things: spending should be channelled where the marginal health benefit per dollar is highest.

Equity vs. Efficiency Trade-Off:

  • Horizontal equity: Equal treatment for equal need — two patients with the same condition should receive the same care regardless of income, geography, or social status.
  • Vertical equity: Greater resources for greater need — sicker patients or those with greater barriers to access should receive proportionally more resources.

In practice, pursuing equity often conflicts with pure efficiency, and every healthcare system must find its own balance.
