1.1 — What Is Public Finance?

Chapter 1: Introduction — Economic Rationale for the Government

What is Public Finance?

Definition

Public finance (also called public economics) is the part of economics that studies the government's role in the economy. It looks at how governments get money (mainly through taxes) and how they spend it (public goods, social help, infrastructure). It also studies how these choices change people's lives and how markets work.

Why is this field so important? Because the government is not far away — it is involved in almost every part of economic life. When you pay taxes on your salary, visit a public hospital, drive on a public road, or get a pension later — the government is there. Knowing public finance means understanding the reasons behind all these decisions.

In practice, we use three main questions to study public finance. These three approaches are the core of the course, and you can see every topic through them.

The Three Fundamental Questions

Question 1

🔍 Positive

How do government policies actually affect the economy?

This approach describes reality: we look at what really happens when a policy starts. We don't judge if the result is "good" or "bad" — we just observe and measure facts.

Example: "Do higher income taxes make people work less?" — This is a fact we can measure.

Question 2

⚖️ Normative

How should policies be made to improve society's well-being?

This is about giving advice: based on facts, what is the best policy? This means we have to make judgments — deciding what "well-being" is and who needs it the most.

Example: "Should the highest tax rate be 50% or 70%?" — The answer depends on your ideas about fairness, efficiency, and freedom.

Question 3

🏛️ Political Economy

Why do governments choose to act the way they do?

Even if we know the "best" policy, governments often do something else. Why? Because of politics, lobbying, what voters want, rules, and politicians' own interests.

Example: "Why do governments help farming even when economists say it's bad?" — Because farmers are an active group in politics.

🎯 Key insight: Every debate in public finance is about these three questions. First, we see what is (positive). Then, we discuss what should be (normative). Finally, we explain why reality is different from the goal (political economy). Knowing this difference is very important for the exam.

Positive Public Economics

Positive Analysis

Positive public economics analyzes how things really are. It uses facts, data, and evidence — not opinions or judgments. The goal is to see the real effects of government policies on the economy and on people's actions.

This part of the field is based on data. It uses math and statistics to find cause-and-effect links. For example, an economist can study a tax change in one region to see how taxes change the way people work.

Positive public economics connects with other fields:

  • Behavioral economics — because people don't always act rationally; they react to psychology and how choices look.
  • Labor economics — because work taxes directly change salaries, work hours, and finding a job.
  • Political economy — because knowing why policies exist helps us understand their results.
  • International tax — because today, money and workers can travel to other countries to pay less taxes.

An important idea in positive analysis is the difference between direct effects and indirect (or unintended) effects. When the government adds a tax on sweet drinks, the direct effect is that the price goes up and people buy less. But there can be indirect effects: consumers might buy artificial sweeteners (which have other health risks), small shops might lose money, or people might travel to another country to buy cheaper drinks. A good analysis looks at both.

⚠️ Important: Positive analysis is a needed first step before normative analysis. You cannot choose what should be done without first knowing what is happening. This is why data research is the base of good policy making.

Normative Public Economics

Normative Analysis

Normative public economics analyzes how things should be. It asks: with what we know about the economy, what is the best policy? Unlike positive economics, normative economics involves personal judgments — about fairness, justice, freedom, and well-being.

This part is mainly theoretical. It uses mathematical models to find the perfect policies. For example, a normative economist tries to find the "best tax rates" — the rates that improve society the most — based on how people react to taxes (facts from positive analysis).

There is a big debate in normative economics:

Approach A

🗽 Laissez-Faire (Free Market)

People know what is best for themselves. The government should not do much and should let markets work freely. People are rational, and their choices show what they really want.

Result: Low taxes, few rules, small government.

Approach B

🏛️ Paternalism (Protection)

People sometimes make bad choices for themselves (smoking, not saving for retirement, ignoring health). The government should push or force people to act differently to protect them and improve their lives.

Result: Higher taxes on "bad goods" (cigarettes), mandatory pensions, public healthcare.

Which approach is "right"? It depends on your main beliefs: do you think people are rational? Is well-being just what people want, or what is good for them? Should we try to have equal results or equal chances? The answer changes everything.

Normative economics also studies two failures that create a need for the government:

  • Market failures — when markets do not give efficient results (pollution, public goods, bad information). These are the normal reasons for the government to help.
  • Individual failures — when people do not do what is best for them, even if markets work well (lack of control, bad habits, waiting too long). These are the new reasons for paternalism (protection).

In reality, the difference between positive and normative economics is not always easy to see. Many debates mix facts ("Do minimum wages create unemployment?") with values ("Is some unemployment fine if the poorest get higher salaries?"). Learning to separate these two is a great skill for this course.

Should the Government Intervene?

The government should not do everything, and the market should not do everything alone. In reality, most people agree that the government needs to act, but debate how much. We can group this into three categories:

Category 1

✅ Almost Everyone Agrees: YES

These are things that almost everyone agrees the government must do. They usually need state power, large organization, or are natural monopolies.

Examples: Military, police, justice system, laws, diplomacy, visas, counting people (census), birth papers, land records, company rules, driving licenses.

Category 2

🔄 Open for Debate

These are the topics people debate a lot. People disagree about whether the government should help, and by how much. This is the main topic of our course.

Examples: Healthcare, pensions, help for jobless people, giving money to the poor, subsidies for trains, schools, housing rules.

Category 3

❌ Almost Everyone Agrees: NO

These are things that everyone agrees the government should not control. They are private choices that do not hurt others or break the market.

Examples: What clothes to wear, whether to eat meat, sleeping hours, if you do sports.

Thinking Method: When you study if the government should act, use this structure:
① Find the market failure (if there is one) — is it an externality, public good, bad information, or a competition issue?
② Check the size — how bad is the problem from this market failure?
③ Think about government failure — could the government create new problems (lobbying, bad information, high costs)?
④ Look at alternatives — is there a private solution or a smaller action (a "nudge") that could work?
This 4-step method is much better than just saying "yes" or "no".

Why Study Public Finance?

There are three good reasons to study public finance:

  1. Practical Importance

    The government is a huge part of any modern economy. For example: the Czech Republic has about 18,000 public groups — schools, hospitals, cities, transport agencies — and employs about 450,000 people directly (and 1 million if you count state companies). This is a big part of the economy and it changes everyone's life.

    In rich countries (OECD), tax money is about 34% of GDP. This means the government controls about one-third of the economy. And this size has grown a lot in the past 100 years — at the start of the 20th century, it was generally under 10%. Understanding why the government grew so much is a main question of public finance.

    Government spending is often equal or higher than tax money, which means many governments spend more than they have (budget deficits). This creates questions about public debt, long-term safety, and fairness for the next generations.

  2. Impact of Policies

    Public finance has real, direct effects on millions of people. When a leader creates help programs, unemployment money, or a tax system, it changes people's income, health, chances, and life quality.

    The main question is: What can leaders do to improve society's well-being? But first we must ask: How do we measure well-being? Is it GDP (money) per person? Is it how the poorest people live? Is it general happiness? Different measures lead to different ideas for policies.

  3. Philosophy & Politics

    At a deeper level, public finance is about political philosophy. It asks: When is the government the best solution? In which areas? Can people take care of themselves (Free Market), or do they need protection (Paternalism)?

    It also studies redistributing wealth and inequality: Should we care about inequality of results (how much money people have at the end) or inequality of opportunity (if everyone starts with a fair chance)? And how should we decide together what the government does — through voting, direct democracy, or experts?

As an economics student, you will see public finance in your career if you work in the government, consulting, charities, or the private sector. Tax rules change business decisions. Public spending changes demand and jobs. Social help changes how much people work. Knowing these links gives you an advantage in any economic job.
