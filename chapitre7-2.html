Chapter 7: Health Economics — Theory, Systems, and the Cost of Aging

Insurance: Expected Utility Theory Applied to Health

To understand health insurance, we apply the standard expected utility framework. Consider a person with income $W$ (regardless of health status), who faces a probability $p$ of becoming sick. If sick, they incur a medical cost $d$ to recover.

Insurance Contract: The individual pays a premium $t$ in all states (healthy or sick), and receives a payout $b$ only if sick.

Expected Utility:

$E(U) = p \cdot U(W - t - d + b) + (1 - p) \cdot U(W - t)$

where $U'' < 0$ (the individual is risk-averse — concave utility function).

Actuarially Fair Insurance:

Insurers' expected profits: $EP = t - p \cdot b$

Competition among insurers drives expected profits to zero: $EP = 0$, which implies:

$b = \frac{t}{p}$

This is called actuarially fair insurance — the premium exactly equals the expected payout. The actuarially fair premium is therefore $t = p \cdot b$ (the payout multiplied by the probability of needing it).

Why Do People Buy (or Not Buy) Insurance?

Individuals face uncertain health states with associated consumption levels. Whether they choose to buy insurance depends on their risk preferences and the pricing of the contract.

Risk-Averse Agents Buy

A risk-averse agent (concave utility, $U'' < 0$) will pay a premium above the actuarially fair price to avoid the uncertainty of a large medical bill. The maximum excess they will pay above the fair price is called the risk premium.

Loading Costs Push Some Out

In practice, real premiums exceed the actuarially fair price because insurers add loading costs — administrative overhead, profit margins, and marketing. These loading costs can push low-risk individuals out of the market, because the premium exceeds their risk premium. When healthy individuals exit → adverse selection begins.

Adverse Selection — The Unravelling Problem

Adverse selection is the most fundamental insurance market failure. It follows directly from Akerlof's (1970) "lemons" logic: individuals who know they are most likely to need care are the ones who disproportionately buy insurance.

The "Death Spiral" Dynamic:

  1. In a pooling equilibrium, the insurer charges a single average premium to all customers (healthy and sick).
  2. This average premium is too high for healthy individuals (they are net losers) and too low for sick individuals (they are net winners). Healthy individuals begin to leave the pool.
  3. As healthy people exit, the pool becomes sicker on average → the insurer must raise premiums.
  4. Higher premiums cause more healthy people to exit → premiums rise again → the cycle continues.
  5. In the worst case, this "death spiral" leads to complete market collapse — only the sickest remain, and no affordable insurance product exists.
📚 Theory: Rothschild-Stiglitz (1976) showed that a separating equilibrium can exist — insurers offer a menu of contracts that induce self-selection (high-deductible/low-premium for healthy; low-deductible/high-premium for sick). However, this equilibrium is potentially inefficient and may not always exist.

Moral Hazard — The Behavioural Response to Coverage

Once insured, individuals change their behaviour in ways that increase costs. This is the moral hazard problem — insurance distorts incentives.

Ex-Ante Moral Hazard

Insured individuals invest less in prevention — diet, exercise, safety precautions. If the cost of illness is partly borne by the insurer, the individual has less incentive to stay healthy. This shifts the health production function downward.

Ex-Post Moral Hazard

Insurance lowers the effective price at point of use, leading to increased utilisation. Patients consume more healthcare because their out-of-pocket cost is far below the actual social cost of the services they receive.

Without insurance, the patient faces the true cost ($100) and consumes the efficient quantity $Q_1$ (point A). With insurance and a low co-payment (~$10), consumption rises to $Q_2$ (point C). Between $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, the social cost of care exceeds the social benefit, creating a deadweight loss triangle. This illustrates the fundamental trade-off: insurance increases access but also generates overconsumption.

Empirical Evidence: RAND and the Czech Experiment

Two landmark experiments provide the strongest empirical evidence on moral hazard in health insurance:

🇺🇸 RAND Health Insurance Experiment (1974–82)

The RAND experiment is the gold standard of health economics research. Participants were randomly assigned to insurance plans with varying co-payment rates (from 0% to 95%).

Key Finding: More generous coverage increased healthcare utilisation by approximately 30%. However, most of the increase was low-value care — there were no significant health outcome differences for most people. The only exception: the poorest and sickest individuals did benefit from more generous coverage.

🇨🇿 Czech Co-payment Experiment (2008–15)

In 2008, the Czech Republic introduced a 90 CZK fee (approximately €3.50) for each GP visit and emergency attendance.

Key Findings:

  • Unscheduled emergency attendances fell by 41% in 2008 compared to 2007.
  • A significant decrease in GP visits was observed, particularly for people aged 50+.
  • No statistically significant effect on hospitalisations — people still went to hospital when truly ill.

Insurance: Provider Payment Methods

How insurers pay providers creates powerful incentive structures that shape healthcare delivery. The general formula for physician payment is:

$P = \alpha + \beta \cdot c$

where $\alpha$ = fixed payment for a given diagnosis, $\beta$ = payment proportional to costs $c$ (tests, nursing, etc.).

Fee-for-Service ($\alpha = 0,\; \beta > 1$)

There is no fixed payment for practice, but the insurance company pays the full cost of all services rendered plus a surcharge. The provider earns more by doing more — this leads to excessive care (supplier-induced demand).

Capitation ($\alpha > 0,\; \beta = 0$)

Payments are made for the number and type of patients, but not for services rendered. The provider earns the same regardless of how much care they provide — this may lead to insufficient care.

DRG System ($\alpha > 0,\; \beta = 1$)

The modern trend: Diagnosis Related Groups (DRG). Patients are sorted into diagnostic groups, and providers receive a fixed payment per diagnosis. This balances the incentive to under-provide (capitation) and over-provide (fee-for-service).

Chapter 7.2 — Insurance Economics:

  • Actuarially fair insurance: $b = t/p$. Risk-averse agents pay a premium above the fair price.
  • Adverse selection (Akerlof 1970): sick buy disproportionately → "death spiral" → market collapse. Solution: mandate.
  • Moral hazard: ex-ante (less prevention) + ex-post (more utilisation). Insurance lowers effective price → overconsumption → deadweight loss.
  • RAND: +30% utilisation, no health benefit for most. Czech: 90 CZK fee → −41% emergency visits.
  • Provider payment: fee-for-service (over), capitation (under), DRG (balanced).
