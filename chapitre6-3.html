6.3 — PAYG vs. Funded Systems

Chapter 6: The Economics of Old-Age Pensions

Pay-As-You-Go (PAYG): The Mechanics

In a PAYG system, current workers finance current retirees. There is no fund that accumulates over time — every euro collected in payroll taxes is immediately transferred to pensioners.

The PAYG Budget Constraint:

$\tau \times w \times N = b \times R$

Where: $\tau$ = contribution rate, $w$ = average wage, $N$ = number of contributors, $b$ = benefit per retiree, $R$ = number of retirees.

Rearranging for the replacement rate:

$\frac{b}{w} = \tau \times \frac{N}{R}$

This means the replacement rate (pension as a share of the average wage) depends directly on the ratio of contributors to retirees. A rising dependency ratio ($N/R$ falling) forces either lower benefits or higher contribution rates — this is the central fiscal tension in ageing societies.

Rate of Return in a PAYG System

What does a participant actually "earn" by contributing to a PAYG system? The answer requires a simple derivation:

Derivation:

Generation $t$ contributes:

$\tau \times w_t = \tau \times w_0 \times (1+g)^t$

where $g$ = real wage growth.

Generation $t$ receives benefit:

$b_{t+1} = \tau \times w_{t+1} \times \frac{N_{t+1}}{R_{t+1}} = \tau \times w_0 \times (1+g)^{t+1} \times (1+n)$

where $n$ = population growth.

Implied gross return on contribution:

$(1 + r_{PAYG}) \approx (1+n)(1+g) \approx 1 + n + g$

(We can ignore the small cross-term $n \times g$.)

🎯 Key Result: $r_{PAYG} \approx n + g$ — the implicit return on a PAYG system equals population growth ($n$) plus real wage growth ($g$). A larger working population means more contributors funding each retiree; higher real wages enlarge the tax base, allowing more generous benefits.

PAYG Systems: Advantages and Drawbacks

✅ No Transition Cost

A PAYG system can be implemented immediately without requiring decades of capital accumulation — the first generation receives benefits without having contributed in full.

✅ Automatic Risk Pooling

PAYG provides insurance against longevity risk, inflation, and individual capital-market fluctuations through intergenerational transfers. A pensioner's income does not depend on stock prices.

✅ Redistribution

Defined-benefit formulas can link contributions and benefits non-linearly, reducing old-age poverty and compressing lifetime income inequality. Low earners can receive more than they contributed.

✅ Resilience to Financial Crises

Benefits are not directly exposed to stock-market crashes, protecting pensioners from asset-price collapses (e.g. the 2008–09 crisis destroyed funded pension values).

❌ Demographic Sensitivity

The implicit return $n + g$ falls as populations age and productivity growth slows — precisely the conditions prevailing in most advanced economies today.

❌ Political Risk

Promises can be revised downward by future governments. Benefit cuts, means-testing, and retirement-age increases are politically painful but fiscally necessary.

❌ No Capital Accumulation

Contributions are immediately transferred out — they do not build up a productive capital stock, potentially depressing the economy's savings rate.

❌ Ponzi-Scheme Logic

Long-run solvency requires perpetual demographic renewal. A permanently shrinking population renders PAYG unsustainable without continuous reforms.

Funded (Capitalised) Systems: The Mechanics

In a funded system, contributions are invested in financial assets; benefits come from the accumulated savings plus returns.

Key Result:

$r_{funded} \approx r$

where $r$ = real rate of return on invested assets (equities, bonds, real estate, etc.).

Funded systems come in three main variants:

Defined Contribution (DC)

Workers accumulate individual accounts; the pension at retirement equals the account balance (contributions plus investment returns). The individual bears all investment and longevity risk. Examples: Chile (1981), Australian Superannuation, US 401(k).

Defined Benefit (DB) — Funded

The employer (or state) promises a specific benefit formula — typically a share of final or career-average salary times years of service. The sponsor must invest enough assets to meet the obligation. The sponsor bears investment risk. Examples: traditional UK and US occupational pension funds.

Notional Defined Contribution (NDC)

A hybrid: accounts are "notional" (no real fund exists) but contributions earn a return equal to wage-bill growth — essentially PAYG with DC-style transparency. Examples: Sweden (1998), Italy (1995 Dini reform), Poland.

Defined Benefit vs. Defined Contribution: Full Comparison

These are two fundamentally different ways to structure a funded pension promise. Understanding their differences is critical for pension policy analysis.

Dimension Defined Benefit (DB) Defined Contribution (DC)
Promise A specific benefit formula, e.g. $b = \alpha \times \text{years} \times w_{ref}$ where $\alpha \approx 1\text{-}2\%$ is the accrual rate. Only the contribution rate is fixed. The benefit = whatever the accumulated account can finance.
Who bears the risk? The sponsor (employer or government). If assets underperform, the sponsor must top up the fund. The worker. Poor returns or retiring in a downturn directly reduce the pension.
Benefit formula $b = \alpha \times \text{years of service} \times w_{ref}$ $b = \frac{\text{Account Balance}}{\text{Annuity Factor}(LE, r)}$
Portability Low. Rights are hard to transfer when changing employers; early leavers typically lose value. High. The account follows the worker across employers; the balance is always their own.
Redistribution Formula can include redistributive elements (minimum pension, capped accrual rate). None by design. Lifetime income inequality passes directly into pension inequality.
Examples Traditional US & UK occupational pensions; pre-reform Italian and French public schemes. Chilean AFP system (1981), Australian Superannuation, US 401(k).
📊 Global Trend: Most OECD countries have shifted new workers from DB to DC or NDC over the past three decades, transferring longevity and investment risk from the state to the individual. This raises adequacy concerns for low-income workers who cannot afford investment losses.

Funded Systems: Advantages and Drawbacks

✅ Demographic Independence

Returns depend on capital markets, not on the ratio of workers to retirees. A shrinking or ageing population does not directly erode the real rate of return on invested assets.

✅ Capital Accumulation

Contributions build a productive capital stock, potentially raising output and wages over time — a positive externality for the broader economy.

✅ Transparency

In DC systems, account balances are visible; individuals can observe their accrued rights and plan their retirement accordingly.

✅ Higher Potential Returns

Historically, diversified equity portfolios have delivered real returns of 4–7% annually, well above $n + g$ in low-growth economies. This can provide significantly higher pensions for the same contribution rate.

❌ Transition Cost

Shifting from PAYG requires a generation that pays twice — funding both the inherited PAYG liability (current retirees who are owed benefits) and their own personal accounts. This fiscal gap can be politically impossible.

❌ Investment & Longevity Risk

In DC systems, individuals bear market risk (a crash just before retirement) and longevity risk (outliving savings). Neither is easy to insure against.

❌ Inadequate Redistribution

Pure DC systems mirror lifetime earnings inequality into retirement income inequality. There is no automatic floor for low earners — they retire poor.

❌ Financial-System Dependence

Large pension funds create systemic risk; poor governance, mismanagement (e.g. the Maxwell scandal in the UK), or regulatory failure can destroy retirement savings for millions.

Chapter 6.3 — PAYG vs Funded:

  • PAYG earns an implicit return of $r_{PAYG} = n + g$ (population growth + wage growth). It is immediately implementable, redistributive, and resilient to market crashes — but highly sensitive to demographics.
  • Funded systems earn $r_{funded} = r$ (the real return on capital). They are demographically independent and build capital — but carry investment risk, transition costs, and provide no automatic redistribution.
  • DB shifts risk to the sponsor (employer/state); DC shifts risk to the individual. The global trend is a shift from DB to DC, raising concerns about pension adequacy.
  • Which system is better? That depends on whether $n + g$ is above or below $r$ — this is Aaron's Rule, covered in Section 6.4.
