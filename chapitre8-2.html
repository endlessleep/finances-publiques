8.2 — Voting Systems & Arrow's Impossibility Theorem

Chapter 8: Public Choice Theory — The Impossibility of Perfect Democracy

Problem 2: Preference Aggregation

Assuming we somehow know what citizens want (Problem 1), the next challenge is: how do we combine millions of different opinions into a single collective decision?

Consider the classic one-dimensional setting: a government must decide on the optimal quantity of public goods or the optimal tax rate. Even in this simple case — where everyone is choosing from a single line of options (e.g. "tax rate between 0% and 100%") — aggregating preferences is already difficult. In real politics, the problem is infinitely harder because decisions are multidimensional: redistribution, immigration policy, moral values, national identity, urban vs. rural priorities, and dozens of other factors all matter simultaneously.

Preference aggregation has two sub-problems:

2a: Decision Rule

What voting rule do we use? Unanimity? Simple majority? Qualified majority? Each rule has different strengths and weaknesses, and no single rule is perfect for every situation.

2b: Translating Votes into Mandates

Once people vote, how do we convert their votes into parliamentary seats? This is largely a mathematical problem, but the math has enormous political consequences (covered in section 8.3).

Problem 2a: Decision Rules

Every collective decision needs an ex ante-defined rule — a clear agreement on how votes will be counted before the voting begins. The choice of rule is not neutral: different rules favor different groups and produce different outcomes. Let's examine the main options:

💡 Key Principle: The optimal rule can differ by context. A simple majority is elegant for everyday decisions, but stronger rules may be desirable for constitutional or irreversible decisions because they protect minorities and ensure broad agreement.

The Six Main Voting Systems

System 1

🤝 Unanimity Voting

Rule: 100% of all agents must agree. Every single person has a full veto right — if even one person says "no", the proposal is rejected.

When it's used: Some international organizations (like the UN Security Council, where permanent members have veto power), and some partnership agreements.

The Good: Perfect protection for minorities. Nobody can ever be forced into something against their will.

Problems: It is extremely rigid. Any single stubborn person can block everything, even if 99% of people want it. It also creates a binary 0/1 nature — you can only say "yes" or "no" to a fixed proposal, with no room for compromise or gradual adjustment.

System 2

✋ Simple Majority Voting

Rule: At least 50%+1 of all agents must agree. The option that gets more than half the votes wins.

When it's used: Most everyday parliamentary decisions, simple referendums, board votes.

The Good: Elegant, simple, fast. It prevents the "tyranny of the minority" where a small group blocks everything.

Problems: Needs an odd number of agents to avoid ties. The majority can "tyrannize" the minority (51% can constantly overrule 49%). It also has 0/1 nature and might not lead to a stable equilibrium (as we will see with cycling).

System 3

🏆 Qualified Majority Voting

Rule: A "supermajority" of $X\%$ must agree, where $X \in (50, 100)$. Common thresholds: 60%, two-thirds (66.7%), or three-quarters (75%).

When it's used: Constitutional amendments (changing a country's fundamental law), EU Council votes on sensitive issues, US Senate override of presidential veto (2/3).

The Good: Offers a middle ground between unanimity (too rigid) and simple majority (too easy to change things). Protects minorities without completely paralyzing the system.

Problems: Requires the total number of agents to be divisible by the chosen denominator ($3x$ for 2/3 majority). Still has 0/1 binary nature. Still might not lead to a stable equilibrium.

System 4

🔄 Sequence of Votes

Rule: Voting happens in multiple rounds, with options being eliminated progressively. Common in presidential elections with two rounds ("runoff").

When it's used: French presidential elections (first round with all candidates, second round between top two), party leadership contests.

The Good: Allows voters to express more nuanced preferences. In the first round, you "vote with your heart" (for your real favorite). In the second round, you "vote with your head" (against your least favorite).

Problems: Very sensitive to setup — the order in which options are presented can change the outcome. It encourages strategic voting (voting not for your first choice, but against someone else). Still 0/1 in each step.

System 5

📊 Ranking and Ordering

Rule: Instead of choosing just one option, voters rank all alternatives from best to worst. The option with the highest sum of ranks wins (Borda count method).

When it's used: Eurovision Song Contest, some academic elections, sports rankings.

The Good: Captures much more information about voter preferences than a simple binary vote. Can avoid the "tyranny of the majority" by considering second and third choices.

Problems: Might not lead to a stable equilibrium. All alternatives get the same weight regardless of how strongly you feel about them. Voters might not correctly observe their own full ranking. Much more complex to understand and implement.

System 6

👍👎 Combined Approval Voting (Democracy 2.1)

Rule: For each option, voters can cast a vote FOR, a vote AGAINST, or express INDIFFERENCE. This captures not just support, but also active opposition.

When it's used: Some participatory budgeting experiments, the "Prezident21" project in the Czech Republic for presidential elections.

The Good: Solves a critical problem with simple majority voting: it reveals that some options may have strong support BUT also strong opposition.

Problems: Complex to implement and explain. Still vulnerable to strategic voting (voting "against" everyone except your favorite).

Democracy 2.1 in Action — The Swimming Pool Example

Imagine a city council must decide which two projects to build using public money (all projects cost the same). There are several options: a swimming pool, a park, a library, etc. Under simple majority voting, each person votes for their favorite project, and the two projects with the most votes win.

But here's the problem: suppose the swimming pool gets the most votes, and so it is built. However, if we looked deeper, we would discover that while 35% of people voted for the pool, a massive 55% actively hate the idea (they think it's a waste of money, or they live far away). Under simple majority, this opposition is completely invisible.

❌ Simple Majority Result

The swimming pool wins because it has the most first-choice votes. But most citizens are actually against it. The system produced a decision that a majority of people oppose.

✅ Democracy 2.1 Result

Under approval voting, the swimming pool gets many "against" votes. A less controversial project (one that most people find acceptable, even if fewer people love it as #1) wins instead. The result better represents the collective preference.

🔑 The Lesson: Simple majority voting can produce results that a majority of people are unhappy with. More sophisticated systems like approval voting or combined approval voting try to solve this by capturing not just support, but also opposition. However, perfect solutions remain just as elusive as ever — which brings us to Arrow's fundamental discovery.

Arrow's Impossibility Theorem: The Death of Perfect Democracy

In 1951, the brilliant mathematician and economist Kenneth Arrow asked a seemingly simple question: Can we design a perfect voting system? He defined what "perfect" means by listing 4 characteristics that any ideal political mechanism should have:

  1. Transitivity

    If society prefers A over B, and society prefers B over C, then society must prefer A over C. This seems obvious — it just means society's preferences are logically consistent and do not go in circles.

    Formally: If $A \succ B$ and $B \succ C$, then $A \succ C$.

  2. Non-Dictatorial Choice

    The system must be genuinely democratic. There should be no single person whose individual preference automatically becomes society's preference. If one person always gets their way regardless of what everyone else thinks, that's a dictatorship, not a democracy.

  3. Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA)

    If society must choose between A and B, the result should depend only on how people rank A vs. B — not on whether there is some third option C. Think of it this way: if you prefer pizza over pasta, introducing sushi to the menu should not suddenly make you prefer pasta over pizza. The same logic should apply to collective decisions.

    Real-world violation: In the 2000 US presidential election, Ralph Nader (the "irrelevant" third-party candidate) took enough votes from Al Gore to let George W. Bush win. The presence of C changed the outcome between A and B.

  4. Unrestricted Domain

    The mechanism must work for all possible combinations of individual preferences. You cannot design a system that only works when people happen to agree, or only works when preferences have a specific shape. The system must be universally robust.

Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1951)

Kenneth Arrow proved mathematically that no voting system can satisfy all four characteristics at the same time.

This is not a matter of bad engineering or lack of creativity. It is a mathematical impossibility. No matter how clever you are, you cannot build a perfectly democratic, perfectly consistent, perfectly independent voting system that works in all situations. Democracy is inherently imperfect.

But hope is not completely lost!

Arrow's theorem applies when all four conditions must hold simultaneously. If we relax the fourth condition (unrestricted domain), things improve dramatically:

  • If preferences are single-peaked (meaning each voter has one ideal point and their satisfaction decreases as you move away from it in either direction), then the majority voting equilibrium always exists.
  • Single-peaked preferences occur naturally when we deal with one issue at a time — like choosing a single tax rate, or deciding how much to spend on education.
  • However, multi-peaked preferences do exist in reality. Example: rich families might either want excellent private schools (low taxes, no public funding) OR excellent public schools (high taxes, generous public funding) — but NOT mediocre public schools (medium taxes). This creates two peaks in their preference curve.
  • When preferences are multi-peaked or multidimensional, a cyclical pattern can emerge: society prefers A over B, B over C, but C over A. This is called a Condorcet cycle, and it means no stable majority winner exists.

The Median Voter Theorem: Who Really Decides?

When preferences are single-peaked and we use majority voting, there is a beautiful and powerful result: the outcome is always the one preferred by the median voter — the person who sits exactly in the middle of the preference distribution.

Classic Example

Suppose 5 citizens must decide how much to spend on public education. Each person has their ideal spending level:

Voter Lucy Tom Jim John Jill
Ideal Spending $0 $840 $1,000 $1,200 $16,000
Position Far Left Center-Left MEDIAN Center-Right Far Right

If we let all five people vote between any pair of proposals using simple majority, Jim's preference ($1,000) always wins. Here is the intuition:

  • $1,000 vs. $840? Jim, John, and Jill all prefer $1,000 or higher → $1,000 wins (3 vs. 2).
  • $1,000 vs. $1,200? Jim, Tom, and Lucy all prefer $1,000 or lower → $1,000 wins (3 vs. 2).
  • $1,000 vs. $16,000? Everyone except Jill prefers $1,000 → $1,000 wins (4 vs. 1).

No matter which alternative you propose, the median voter's ideal point always beats it in a pairwise vote. This is the median voter theorem, and it has a profound implication for politics:

🎯 The Hotelling–Downs Prediction: In a one-dimensional political competition, both parties should converge toward the median voter's position. If Party A positions itself at the median and Party B does not, Party A will always steal the majority. This is why, in theory, left-wing and right-wing parties should eventually become very similar — they both chase the same median voter.

Political Polarization: Why Parties Don't Converge

The median voter theorem predicts that parties should converge to the center. But look at any newspaper today and you will see the exact opposite: politics is becoming increasingly polarized. Left-wing parties are moving more to the left, right-wing parties are moving more to the right, and the "center" is shrinking. Why does reality so violently contradict the theory?

  1. Politics Is Multidimensional

    The median voter theorem only works perfectly in one dimension (e.g. "more taxes vs. less taxes"). But real politics involves dozens of dimensions simultaneously: economic redistribution, immigration, cultural values, identity, geography, foreign policy. When politics is multidimensional, there is no single median — and no guaranteed equilibrium.

  2. Parties Respond to Activists, Not Just Voters

    Political parties need volunteers, donors, and passionate members to survive. These activists tend to hold much more extreme views than the average voter. Parties therefore move toward the extremes to keep their base energized, even if it means losing a few centrist voters.

  3. Turnout Is Endogenous

    If a party moves to the center, its core supporters might feel betrayed and stay home on election day. Conversely, taking a strong, extreme position mobilizes your base. In many elections, mobilizing extremes is a more effective strategy than persuading the center.

  4. Electoral Systems Matter

    Under proportional representation (like in Czechia), small extreme parties can win seats. This gives voters an incentive to vote for niche parties, and it encourages fragmentation and coalition-building rather than centrist convergence.

  5. Politicians Have Their Own Ideology

    The Downs model assumes politicians are purely power-seekers with no personal beliefs. But in reality, many politicians genuinely believe in their extreme positions. They enter politics specifically because they want to change the world according to their vision.

⚠️ Conclusion: The median voter theorem is an extremely useful theoretical benchmark, but in realistic settings it does not prevent pressures for polarization. In fact, many of the strongest forces in modern politics actively push parties away from the center.

Chapter 8.2 — The Big Picture:

  • Preference aggregation = how to combine millions of different opinions into one decision. Two sub-problems: decision rules and translating votes into mandates.
  • Six main voting systems: Unanimity, Simple Majority, Qualified Majority, Sequence of Votes, Ranking, Combined Approval. Each has fatal flaws.
  • Democracy 2.1 (approval voting) solves the problem of simple majority producing results that most people oppose, by capturing both support AND opposition.
  • Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1951): No voting system can simultaneously satisfy Transitivity, Non-Dictatorship, Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, and Unrestricted Domain.
  • The escape: If preferences are single-peaked (one issue, one ideal point), majority voting gives a stable equilibrium → the Median Voter Theorem.
  • Hotelling–Downs: In one dimension, both parties should converge to the median. But polarization happens anyway because of multidimensionality, activist pressure, endogenous turnout, and politicians' own ideology.
