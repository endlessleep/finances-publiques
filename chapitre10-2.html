10.2 — Ramsey Taxation & Taxing Commodities

Chapter 10: Optimal Taxation & Personal Income Tax — The Efficiency Angle

Starting from Efficiency: Lessons from Lecture 9

Before turning to the full optimal tax problem (which involves both equity and efficiency), it is useful to isolate the pure efficiency angle. What does minimizing welfare loss tell us about how to tax commodities?

Recall from Chapter 9.2 the two key lessons on efficiency:

Lesson 1

📊 Tax Many Things a Little

Rather than taxing a few goods at very high rates, it is more efficient to spread a small tax over many goods. DWL grows with the square of the tax rate — so doubling the rate quadruples the loss. Broad-base, low-rate systems minimize total deadweight loss.

Caveat: Very broad bases increase administrative complexity and compliance costs.

Lesson 2

🎯 Tax Inelastic Goods

Goods with inelastic demand generate less DWL per euro of revenue when taxed, because consumers cannot easily substitute away. The behavioral response (quantity reduction) is small regardless of the tax rate.

Caveat: Inelastic goods are often necessities (food, energy, medicine) — taxing them is regressive.

Now: How exactly should we set the tax rate? The answer comes from the Ramsey (1927) framework, which formalizes Lesson 2 into a precise rule.

The Ramsey Rule (1927): Inverse Elasticity

The Ramsey Problem

Setting: Only commodity taxes are available (no income tax). Only linear (proportional) taxes are allowed. The government must raise a fixed revenue $R_0$.

Optimization problem: Choose tax rates $t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_N$ for each good $i$ to:

$\min_{t_i} \; DWL(t_1, \ldots, t_N) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i t_i \cdot q_i \geq R_0$

This is solved using the Lagrange multiplier method to find the constrained minimum of deadweight loss.

The Ramsey Rule (Result)

The optimal tax rate on good $i$ is inversely proportional to the uncompensated elasticity of demand:

$\frac{t_i}{p_i} = \frac{k}{\eta_i^u}$

where $k$ is a constant (the same for all goods, determined by the revenue constraint) and $\eta_i^u$ is the uncompensated demand elasticity for good $i$.

Reading the rule: The lower the elasticity (more inelastic demand), the higher the optimal tax rate. The higher the elasticity (elastic demand), the lower the optimal rate. Tax rates should be set inversely to how responsive demand is.

🔑 Intuition: A tax on an inelastic good causes little behavioral change — consumers must buy regardless of price. The revenue is collected with minimal DWL. A tax on an elastic good causes large substitution — consumers flee the taxed good, DWL is high, and revenue is low. The Ramsey rule says: target the goods that people cannot avoid buying.

The Regressivity Problem

The Ramsey rule is elegant — but it immediately runs into a severe equity problem.

The Problem

Demand for necessities (food, basic energy, medicines, housing) is highly inelastic — poor and rich alike must purchase them regardless of price. By the Ramsey rule, we should tax necessities at high rates.

But necessities constitute a much larger share of poor households' income than rich households' income. A high tax on bread is essentially a regressive tax — it hits the poor harder in percentage terms. Pure efficiency reasoning points directly toward regressive taxation.

Three practical solutions address this conflict:

  1. Allow for Different Elasticities by Income Group

    In principle, we could recognize that the elasticity of demand for necessities differs across income groups — a poor person may have truly no alternative to buying bread, while a rich person could substitute expensive substitutes. Conditioning taxes on income-group elasticities would be more equitable. In practice, however, this is extremely difficult to implement — we cannot charge different prices for the same good based on buyer income (without means-testing at every cashier).

  2. Implement Exemptions on Necessities

    The simplest and most widespread solution: apply reduced or zero tax rates on necessities. This sacrifices some efficiency (since necessities are inelastic, we forego easy revenue) to improve equity (poor households' consumption basket is lighter). This is exactly what reduced VAT rates on food, medicine, and books achieve.

  3. Complement Commodity Taxation with Income Taxation

    The most powerful solution: use commodity taxes for efficiency gains, and income taxes for redistribution. A uniform VAT at 21% is efficient and easy to collect; a progressive income tax corrects for the regressivity of the VAT. The two systems work together, each doing what it is best at. This is the dominant approach in developed economies.

For the exam: The Ramsey rule → inverse elasticity → tax necessities more → regressivity problem → three solutions. This is a complete chain of logic. Know it fluently. The examiner will expect you to identify the tension between the efficiency rule and equity, and to explain why real-world VAT systems use reduced rates for food and medicine.

Commodity Taxation in Practice: VAT in Czechia

In Czechia and most of the developed world, the primary commodity tax is Value-Added Tax (VAT). It is economically the most significant tax — accounting for over 20% of total government revenue.

The Czech VAT system applies differentiated rates to address the regressivity concern identified by the Ramsey framework:

Rate Category Applies To Economic Rationale
21% Standard Most goods and services Broad base; goods with more elastic demand (luxuries, discretionary spending)
12% Reduced Food, books, medicines, hotel accommodation, public transport Necessities, especially those consumed by lower-income households; reduces regressivity
0% Zero-Rated Exports, intra-EU supplies Avoid double taxation in international trade (VAT charged at destination)
Exempt No VAT Financial services, education, healthcare No input tax credit; complex to VAT; often subsidized for equity reasons
🌍 International Comparison: The lecture's first graph ("Taxes on income vs. taxes on goods and services, 2022") reveals a global pattern. Developing countries rely more heavily on consumption taxes (VAT, sales taxes) because informal employment makes income harder to observe, while VAT is collected at the point of sale with fewer evasion opportunities. Developed European countries tend to have both high income taxes and high consumption taxes — reflecting a large fiscal capacity and a powerful state. The US is an outlier — relatively high income taxes but no federal VAT due to political resistance, relying more on state-level sales taxes.

Connecting the Macro and Micro Views of Taxation

The two graphs from the lecture complement each other and together reveal the full picture of any tax system:

Macro View

📊 Income vs. Consumption Taxes (Cross-Country)

This graph shows how countries choose their tax base. No single model dominates. The fundamental trade-off is:

  • Income taxes = equity (progressive structure, ability-to-pay)
  • Consumption taxes = efficiency (easier to collect, broad base, less behavioral distortion of labor supply)

Real systems are a mix, determined by administrative capacity, political economy, and distributional goals.

Micro View

📈 Marginal vs. Average Tax Rate (US)

This graph shows how the progressive structure works at the individual level. The marginal rate (red) rises in steps (tax brackets); the average rate (blue) rises smoothly and is always below marginal.

Critical insight: Behavioral responses (how much to work, whether to evade, whether to invest) depend on the marginal rate, not the average rate. This is why Mirrlees and Saez focus on the marginal rate schedule as the main design variable.

⚠️ The Policy Synthesis: Governments face two simultaneous choices: (1) tax base — income or consumption? and (2) tax structure — progressive or flat? The Ramsey framework helps answer question (1) for commodity taxation. Mirrlees and Saez (Chapter 10.3) answer question (2) for income taxation. Real systems combine both instruments.

Chapter 10.2 — The Big Picture:

  • Efficiency lessons: Tax many things a little (DWL ∝ t²); tax inelastic goods (less behavioral response).
  • Ramsey (1927): $\frac{t_i}{p_i} = \frac{k}{\eta_i^u}$ — optimal tax is inversely proportional to demand elasticity. Minimize DWL subject to revenue constraint.
  • Regressivity problem: Necessities are inelastic → taxed heavily by Ramsey → but necessities = large share of poor income → regressive.
  • Three solutions: (1) differentiate by income group [impractical], (2) exemptions on necessities [Czech VAT reduced rates], (3) complement with progressive income tax [the actual solution].
  • Czech VAT: 21% standard / 12% reduced (food, medicine, books) / 0% exports / exempt (finance, health, education).
  • Marginal vs. average rate: Behavior depends on marginal rate. Average rate tells you the total burden. Never confuse them.
