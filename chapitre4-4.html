4.4 — Protecting the Environment

Chapter 4: Public Goods & Externalities

A Global Challenge: How Much to Reduce?

Economic debates today focus on how much to reduce pollution, not whether externalities exist. Since activities create both costs and benefits, we must find the optimal level.

Nature of Environmental Externalities:

  • Global: Climate change affects everyone, regardless of borders.
  • Diffuse: Damage is not localized in one spot.
  • Long-term: Effects last for centuries.

➡️ Conclusion: We need international cooperation, as national solutions are insufficient.

Historical Awareness: The Great Smog (1952)

Before the mid-20th century, air was "taken for granted." The Great Smog of London changed this: estimates show 12,000 deaths and 200,000 injured (dramatized in The Crown, S01E04). Awareness grew as the environment became a scarce resource.

Other Environmental Frontiers:

It's not just CO2. We see similar externalities in:

  • Water & Land: Toxic waste, pesticides, and hormonal birth control (affecting aquatic life).
  • Atmosphere: Ozone depletion and acid rain.

CO2 Emissions: Empirical Trends

History shows a direct link between industrial growth and pollution. We can identify three phases:

1. Before 1900

Emissions were near zero. The economy was overwhelmingly agricultural, energy came from wood and animal power, and industry was limited to a few regions of Britain and Western Europe.

2. 1900 — 1950

Moderate increases as early industrialization spread through Europe and North America. Coal-powered factories and railways began to leave a measurable carbon footprint, though still relatively small by modern standards.

3. Post-1950 Explosion

A massive, exponential spike in emissions globally. This "Great Acceleration" was driven by rapid economic growth, mass motorisation, urbanisation, and near-total dependence on fossil fuel energy (coal, oil, natural gas).

Analysis by Region: The distribution of emissions has shifted dramatically over time:

  • China: Experienced a rapid increase after 2000, driven by its industrialisation and coal-heavy energy mix. China is now the world's largest total emitter, though its per-capita emissions remain lower than the US.
  • USA/Europe: Reached a plateau in the early 2000s and now show a steady decline due to environmental regulations, energy efficiency gains, and the shift from coal to renewables and gas. However, their cumulative historical emissions remain far higher.

Total vs. Per Capita: Who is Responsible?

The "responsibility" for climate change depends on how you measure it:

Option 1: Total Emissions

If you measure by total CO₂ emitted, China is #1. But this is partly a function of size — any country with 1.4 billion people will have high total figures. Large countries always look more "guilty" under this metric, which hides per-person differences.

Option 2: Per Capita (Per Person)

If you measure emissions per person, the picture changes drastically. The USA, Canada, and Australia have some of the highest per-capita emissions in the world, while India and Sub-Saharan Africa are far below the global average. This measure is more equitable and reveals the true "carbon footprint" of individual lifestyles.

The Political Conflict: This creates a fundamental geopolitical divide. Developing nations (China, India) argue that economic growth is essential to lift their populations out of poverty, and that developed nations — which industrialised first and emitted the most historically — should bear the greater burden. Meanwhile, developed nations (US, EU) argue that current emissions matter most and that everyone must cut now. This disagreement has paralysed every major international climate negotiation.

Global Governance: Kyoto & Paris

Since climate change is a global externality, we need global rules.

Kyoto Protocol (1997)

The first major attempt. Focused on quotas for developed countries and carbon permit trading.

Paris Agreement (2015)

Goal: Keep warming below 2°C (ideally 1.5°C). Countries set their own "Nationally Determined Contributions" (NDCs).

Difficulties & Latest Developments:

  • Political Instability: US withdrawal under Trump (2025).
  • Funding Gap: Promises to provide $100B/year to developing nations are often unmet.
  • COP29 Baku (2024): Many observers found the outcome disappointing, highlighting the difficulty of reaching binding global agreements.

COVID-19: A Proof of Reversibility

The 2020 lockdowns provided a massive empirical experiment. When economic activity stopped, pollution dropped instantly.

China & Wuhan Case Study:

  • Satellite data showed NO2 pollution clouds completely disappearing over industrial zones.
  • Lesson: Pollution is reversible, but it requires radical shifts in economic behavior.

Why Coase Fails: The Future Generation

Can the **Coase Theorem** solve climate change? No.

The Intergenerational Problem: Negotiation is impossible because the people most affected (those born in 2100) are not yet alive to bargain.

Economic Solution: We must "decide for them" by using Public Debt to invest in green tech now, with future generations repaying that debt (intertemporal trade-off).

Supplementary Definitions

1. Pecuniary Externality

Only changes prices and profits, not real welfare. (e.g., a factory using more electricity raises prices for others).

2. Common Resource Problem

Consumption of a rival, non-excludable good leads to depletion. (e.g., overfishing, oil reserves).

3. Positional Externality

Value depends on your rank relative to others. (e.g., getting a degree makes others' degrees look less scarce).

🌱

PROTECTING THE FUTURE

  • Global Risk: Climate change is the ultimate global externality — national solutions alone cannot fix a problem that crosses every border. International cooperation is necessary but extremely difficult to achieve.
  • Metric Matters: Whether you measure total emissions or per-capita emissions fundamentally changes who bears the responsibility — and this disagreement is at the heart of every climate negotiation.
  • Unique Awareness: For the first time in Earth's history, a species (us) is aware of its impact on the planet and actually cares about species extinction. This creates both a moral obligation and a political opportunity.
  • Long-term Uncertainty: We still do not fully understand what the long-term consequences of biodiversity loss and environmental degradation will be. This uncertainty is itself an argument for precautionary action.
