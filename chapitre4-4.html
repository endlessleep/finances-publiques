4.4 — Protecting the Environment

Chapter 4: Public Goods & Externalities

A Global Challenge: How Much to Reduce?

Economic debates today focus on how much to reduce pollution, not whether externalities exist. Since activities create both costs and benefits, we must find the optimal level.

Nature of Environmental Externalities:

  • Global: Climate change affects everyone, regardless of borders.
  • Diffuse: Damage is not localized in one spot.
  • Long-term: Effects last for centuries.

➡️ Conclusion: We need international cooperation, as national solutions are insufficient.

Historical Awareness: The Great Smog (1952)

Before the mid-20th century, air was "taken for granted." The Great Smog of London changed this: estimates show 12,000 deaths and 200,000 injured. Awareness grew as the environment became a scarce resource.

CO2 Emissions: Empirical Trends

History shows a direct link between industrial growth and pollution. We can identify three phases:

1. Before 1900

Emissions were near zero. The economy was mostly agricultural and non-industrialized.

2. 1900 — 1950

Moderate increases as early industrialization began in the West.

3. Post-1950 Explosion

A massive spike in emissions globally. Linked to rapid economic growth and fossil fuel energy (coal, oil).

Analysis by Region:

  • China: Rapid increase after 2000; currently the largest total emitter.
  • USA/Europe: Reached a plateau and now show a steady decline due to environmental policies.

Total vs. Per Capita: Who is Responsible?

The "responsibility" for climate change depends on how you measure it:

Option 1: Total Emissions

Chine is #1. Large countries always look more "guilty" because of their sheer size.

Option 2: Per Capita (Per Person)

The USA and Canada have some of the highest emissions per person, while India/Africa are very low.

The Political Conflict: This creates a major debate. Developing nations (like China/India) argue they need to grow, while developed nations (like the US) argue that everyone must cut down now.

Global Governance: Kyoto & Paris

Since climate change is a global externality, we need global rules.

Kyoto Protocol (1997)

The first major attempt. Focused on quotas for developed countries and carbon permit trading.

Paris Agreement (2015)

Goal: Keep warming below 2°C (ideally 1.5°C). Countries set their own "Nationally Determined Contributions" (NDCs).

Difficulties:

  • Political Instability: US withdrawal under Trump (rejoined by Biden, withdrawn again by Trump in 2025).
  • Funding: Promises to provide $100B per year to developing nations often go unmet.

COVID-19: A Proof of Reversibility

The 2020 lockdowns provided a massive empirical experiment. When economic activity stopped, pollution dropped instantly.

China & Wuhan Case Study:

  • Satellite data showed NO2 pollution clouds completely disappearing over industrial zones.
  • Lesson: Pollution is reversible, but it requires radical shifts in economic behavior.

Why Coase Fails: The Future Generation

Can the **Coase Theorem** solve climate change? No.

The Intergenerational Problem: Negotiation is impossible because the people most affected (those born in 2100) are not yet alive to bargain.

Economic Solution: We must "decide for them" by using Public Debt to invest in green tech now, with future generations repaying that debt (intertemporal trade-off).

Supplementary Definitions

1. Pecuniary Externality

Only changes prices and profits, not real welfare. (e.g., a factory using more electricity raises prices for others).

2. Common Resource Problem

Consumption of a rival, non-excludable good leads to depletion. (e.g., overfishing, oil reserves).

3. Positional Externality

Value depends on your rank relative to others. (e.g., getting a degree makes others' degrees look less scarce).

PROTECTING THE FUTURE

  • Global Risk: National solutions can't fix global warming.
  • Metric Matters: Total vs. Per Capita defines who pays.
  • No Coase: Future generations can't sit at the table.
  • Knowledge Gap: We don't know the long-term impact of crowding out nature.
