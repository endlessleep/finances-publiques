6.4 — Aaron's Rule

Chapter 6: The Economics of Old-Age Pensions

Comparing Rates of Return: PAYG vs. Funded

The central question of pension system design is: which system delivers a better lifetime return for participants?

PAYG System

$r_{PAYG} = n + g$

= population growth + real wage growth

Current estimates (EU): $n \approx 0\%\text{--}0.3\%$, $g \approx 0.8\%\text{--}1.2\%$

→ $r_{PAYG} \approx 1\text{--}1.5\%$

Funded System

$r_{funded} = r$

= real return on invested capital

Historical real equity return: ≈ 4–7%

Safe asset (10yr bond, 2024): ≈ 0–2%

💡 Which delivers more? It depends on whether $n + g$ is above or below $r$ — this is the essence of Aaron's condition. Note that this comparison abstracts from risk — funded system returns are riskier, so a risk-adjusted comparison would reduce the funded system's advantage.

The Micro vs. Macro Tension

There is a fundamental conflict between what is good for each individual and what is fiscally sustainable for the whole system:

Microeconomic Optimality

From an individual's perspective, the higher the benefits and the lower the taxes, the better. Each person wants to maximise their personal return on contributions.

Macroeconomic Sustainability

But higher pensions and lower revenues widen the deficit and increase debt over time. What is individually optimal can be collectively unsustainable.

The Government's Dilemma: The government always decides both the contribution rate and the pension benefit — and often changes either or both. Individuals contribute over their working life (for example, 28% of salary in the Czech Republic) and receive a pension benefit after retirement (around 40–45% of their previous wage on average). The benefit formula is always progressive — lower earners get a higher replacement rate than higher earners.

Real-World Returns: Czech Republic and Beyond

How do these theoretical returns compare to actual experience?

Estimated Internal Real Rate of Return on the Czech Pension System (2015):

Income Level Internal Rate of Return Interpretation
50% of average wage +1.5% Low earners receive a positive return — the progressive formula redistributes towards them.
Average wage −1.4% Average earners receive a negative real return — they would be better off investing privately (in theory).
200% of average wage −3.1% High earners face a significant implicit tax — the system strongly redistributes away from them.

Is the Czech IRR Good Enough? That depends on the alternatives and on your aversion to risk:

  • The annual nominal return on the Czech stock exchange (PX index) has been 5.9% since 1993. During the same period, annual inflation averaged 4.3%, giving a real return of about 1.6%.
  • The US S&P 500 has delivered an annual yield of about 8.0% since 1997 (nominal).
  • The S&P Global (World) index has a 10-year annual yield of about 11.5% (nominal).
  • But beware: international investing also carries exchange rate risks — returns in USD or EUR must be converted back into CZK.

Aaron's Rule (1966): The Condition for PAYG Optimality

Henry Aaron published this landmark result in the American Economic Review (1966, Vol. 56, No. 3). It provides the formal condition for when a PAYG system is welfare-improving compared to funded savings.

Aaron's Condition:

PAYG is Pareto-improving $\iff$ $n + g > r$

$n + g > r$ → PAYG Preferred

The "biological return" on social insurance exceeds the market return on capital. Every cohort gains from participating in the PAYG system relative to self-funding. In this case, PAYG is Pareto-improving — everyone is made better off.

$n + g = r$ → Indifferent

This is the "golden rule" of capital accumulation: PAYG and funded systems yield identical welfare. Neither dominates; the choice depends on distributional preferences and risk attitudes.

$n + g < r$ → Funded Preferred

Capital is more productive than population growth can justify. Compulsory PAYG taxes workers at a below-market implicit return, reducing welfare relative to investing in capital markets.

Aaron's Rule in Today's Economies

Does the condition $n + g > r$ hold in practice? Let us check with the latest available data:

Country / Region $n$ (pop. growth) $g$ (real wage growth) $n + g$ $r$ (real safe rate) Aaron condition?
EU-27 (2015–24 avg.) ~0.1% ~0.9% ~1.0% ~0.5–1.5% Borderline
Germany ~0.3% ~0.8% ~1.1% ~0.8% Marginal
Italy ~−0.3% ~0.2% ~−0.1% ~0.8% ✗ Fails
Japan ~−0.4% ~0.3% ~−0.1% ~0.2% Just fails
United States ~0.5% ~1.2% ~1.7% ~1.5% ✓ Holds

What this means for policy:

For Italy and Japan — where demographic decline means $n$ is negative — the Aaron condition fails even at historically low real interest rates. This provides a theoretical rationale for partial funded pillars and explains why both countries have introduced reforms shifting weight toward funded or NDC elements.

For the United States, the condition holds narrowly, but the Congressional Budget Office projects Social Security outlays rising from 5.2% of GDP today to 7.0% by 2097 absent reform — illustrating that holding the Aaron condition is necessary but not sufficient for long-run solvency.

Policy Implications and Reform Directions

From theory to practice — what reform options are available to governments facing an ageing population and a failing Aaron condition?

01

Raise the Retirement Age

As life expectancy rises, keeping benefits constant at a fixed retirement age raises $R$ mechanically. Indexing the retirement age to life expectancy (as Sweden and Finland have done) restores actuarial balance without explicit benefit cuts. This is the simplest and most politically visible reform.

02

NDC: Best of Both Worlds?

Notional defined-contribution accounts (Sweden 1998, Italy 1995) mimic the transparency of DC while retaining the pooling benefits of PAYG. The implicit return is pegged to wage-bill growth — close to $n + g$ — making the Aaron condition operationally visible to participants.

03

Multi-Pillar Architecture

The World Bank (1994) advocated a three-pillar model: (1) mandatory PAYG for redistribution, (2) mandatory funded for savings, (3) voluntary private saving. Diversification across pillars hedges demographic, political, and financial risks simultaneously.

04

Pre-Funding in PAYG

Accumulating a reserve fund — as the US Social Security Trust Fund (partially) and Norway's Government Pension Fund do — can bridge demographic bulges without a full structural shift, buying time for adjustment without permanently abandoning PAYG logic.

Chapter 6 — Key Takeaways

  1. Demographics are destiny for pensions.

    The old-age dependency ratio is the single most powerful driver of pension system finances. The EU-27 ratio is projected to rise from 33% to nearly 57% by 2070, with Japan already at 49%.

  2. PAYG implicitly earns $n + g$.

    The "biological return" on a PAYG system equals population growth plus real wage growth. It is not a market return — it is a political promise backed by demographic trends.

  3. Funded systems earn $r$ — but with risk.

    Capital markets have historically offered higher returns than $n + g$ in advanced economies, but that advantage comes with investment and longevity risk that workers must bear.

  4. Aaron's condition resolves the debate.

    $n + g > r \Rightarrow$ PAYG preferred. In low-growth, ageing economies (Italy, Japan), the condition fails, providing a theoretical basis for partial funding. In faster-growing economies (US), it holds — though barely.

  5. No system is optimal in isolation.

    The academic consensus points toward diversified multi-pillar architectures combining PAYG redistribution with funded savings and parametric reform (retirement-age indexation, NDC transparency).

Chapter 6.4 — Aaron's Rule:

  • Aaron's Condition: PAYG is Pareto-improving if and only if $n + g > r$. This is the single most important result in pension economics.
  • In today's world, the condition fails for Italy and Japan (negative $n$), is borderline for the EU, and just holds for the US.
  • The Czech pension system has a negative real IRR for average and high earners, but a positive one for low earners — reflecting the progressive benefit formula.
  • The mainstream consensus favours multi-pillar architectures — no single system can handle demographic risk, market risk, and political risk simultaneously.
