11.2 — International Tax Reforms

Chapter 11: Tax Avoidance — BEPS, GloBE & The Minimum Tax

The Core Problem & Internal Profit Centers

The fundamental issue in international taxation is that MNCs do not necessarily pay taxes where they truly create value. Because subsidiaries are separate legal entities, they can invoice each other to artificially shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions. This erodes the tax base (Base Erosion) of countries where real economic activity occurs.

Internal Profit Centers

A crucial keyword in this course. An Internal Profit Center is a subsidiary located in a tax haven or offshore financial center that receives massive amounts of money from other subsidiaries within the group.

The key word is internal. The subsidiary does not sell to external customers. It receives royalties, interest, and licensing fees internally. It reports massive profits but has virtually no real substance (no employees, no factories, no physical assets).

The course distinguishes between true operational entities (which sell to real customers) and internal profit centers. To measure this empirically, researchers look at the Effective Tax Rate (ETR): the actual percentage of tax paid on profits after all deductions. Internal profit centers typically feature massive profits combined with extremely low ETRs.

BEPS & The Transparency Push

The first major international response was BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting). BEPS is fundamentally an "anti-abuse" reform. Its goal is to close loopholes and make artificial tax structuring much harder.

Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR)

Historically, tax authorities could only see consolidated group accounts. They knew the MNC made $10 Billion globally, but had no idea where those profits were declared versus where the employees were located. BEPS introduced CbCR to fix this data gap.

📊 Why Data Matters: CbCR forces very large MNCs to report profits, taxes, and employees country by country. If Country A has 10,000 employees but $0 profit, and Country B has 5 employees but $2 Billion profit, tax authorities can easily identify suspected profit shifting. This visibility is essential for empirical research.

Did BEPS work? Yes, but only modestly. BEPS improved transparency and closed loopholes, but it did not remove the fundamental incentive. As long as a country offers a near-zero tax rate, firms will find complex legal ways to shift profits there.

GloBE & The Global Minimum Tax

Because BEPS was insufficient, the international community moved to GloBE (Global Anti-Base Erosion Rules). This represents a massive shift in logic:

  • BEPS: Tries to police the location of profits by closing loopholes.
  • GloBE: Attacks the incentive to shift profits by imposing a Global Minimum Tax of 15%.

GloBE establishes a floor. If a subsidiary in Bermuda pays 0% tax, another country can apply a Top-Up Tax to bring the effective rate up to 15%. This mechanically destroys the massive advantage of tax havens.

The Three GloBE Priority Mechanisms

If a subsidiary pays less than 15%, who gets to collect the Top-Up Tax? The rules establish a strict priority chain:

Priority 1

📍 QDMTT

Source Country Recovers

The country where the low-taxed subsidiary is located can apply a Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax. It's logical: the local country prefers to collect the missing 5% itself rather than letting another country take it.

Priority 2

🏢 IIR

Parent Country Recovers

If the local country does not apply QDMTT, the Income Inclusion Rule allows the country where the MNC's parent headquarters is located to collect the top-up tax.

Priority 3

⚔️ UTPR

Last Resort

If neither acts, the Undertaxed Profits Rule allows other countries where the MNC operates to collect a portion. This is highly controversial politically.

The US "Side-by-Side" Approach & Carve-Outs

The United States complicates the system because it already has its own set of rules for American MNCs. The US refuses to let other countries apply UTPR to American firms. This resulted in the Side-by-Side Agreement: the GloBE system and the US system coexist.

The critical question here is the Level Playing Field. If European firms strictly face a 15% floor, but American firms can sometimes pay 10% or 12% under their parallel system, American firms gain a competitive advantage (more profit to invest, cut prices, or reward shareholders).

Carve-Outs & Deceptive Averages

Simulations show that under the Side-by-Side approach, the macro average ETR might look similar between GloBE and US firms. However, averages are deceptive. A system where every firm pays 15% has the same average as a system where half pay 25% and half pay 5%. The latter is deeply unfair.

Carve-Outs

GloBE includes substance-based Carve-Outs (exemptions). If a firm has real employees and tangible assets in a low-tax country, a portion of its profit is exempt from the top-up tax. This ensures firms aren't penalized for genuine economic activity, but it also means the final effective rate can easily remain below 15%.

Key Takeaways & Exam Conclusion

Perfect Exam Conclusion:
"The course shows that international tax reforms have made profit shifting harder, but not impossible. BEPS improved transparency (CbCR) and closed some loopholes, while GloBE directly reduces the incentive to shift profits by introducing a 15% minimum tax. However, because of substance-based carve-outs, incomplete global implementation, and the U.S. Side-by-Side arrangement, the system is still not fully harmonized and may fail to create a perfectly level playing field."
