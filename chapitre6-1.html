6.1 — Pension Economics: Basics

Chapter 6: The Economics of Old-Age Pensions

The Retirement Problem: How to Transfer Income

Life is long and getting longer. People live longer than they earn income — working roughly 40 years but potentially spending 20+ years in retirement. The central question of pension economics is: how do you guarantee a stable income over such a long period? And more importantly — who do you trust to hold this promise for decades?

Key Insight: Every retirement system, whatever its design, tries to do the same thing — smooth consumption across the life cycle. Current workers need to build a claim on future production. All systems rely on long-term contracts, either implicit (social, political) or explicit (financial, contractual).

The Four Mechanisms: Historically, societies have used four different ways to transfer income from working years to old age:

01

Store Current Production

Set aside physical goods or commodities while working and consume them in old age. This is highly inflexible — goods perish, preferences change, and storage is costly. Rarely used beyond subsistence economies.

02

Raise Children

Invest in the next generation and rely on them for support in old age. This is the oldest and still most common form of retirement provision globally. However, it is sensitive to family circumstances and limits the mobility of younger generations.

03

Accumulate Financial Wealth

Save part of working-age income in financial assets (stocks, bonds, property). Returns depend on capital markets. This requires sophisticated financial infrastructure, financial literacy, and — crucially — rational, far-sighted behaviour from individuals.

04

Accumulate State Pension Rights

Participate in a government-organised pension system. The state acts as the guarantor of an intergenerational contract, replacing the private family contract with a public one. This is the dominant model in advanced economies today.

💡 Implication: Every society uses a different mix of these four mechanisms, and these evolve over time. No system is permanent — reform is a constant feature of pension policy.

Three Models of Retirement Provision

In practice, societies rely on three broad pillars for retirement. Every country blends these differently, reflecting its history, culture, labour-market institutions, and demographic structure.

👨‍👩‍👧 Family

Where: Most developing countries, parts of Asia.

Parents rely on their children for old-age support. This is the oldest and still the most widespread form globally. It requires strong family ties and high fertility. As fertility falls and families become smaller and more geographically dispersed, the family model alone is increasingly insufficient.

📈 Financial Markets

Where: Some OECD, some Asia.

Individuals save during working years and draw down financial assets in retirement. This requires well-developed capital markets, financial literacy, and rational long-run planning. The theoretical ideal — but full of practical problems (see below).

🏛️ Government

Where: Dominant everywhere else.

The state organises and mandates pension provision — either through forced savings (funded DC), pay-as-you-go transfers (PAYG DB), or a mix of both. This is the model we will analyse in the rest of this chapter.

Why Financial Markets Alone Fail

In theory, a rational, far-sighted individual with perfect information would save exactly the right amount during working years to finance their retirement. In practice, four major uncertainties make this extremely difficult:

⏳ Longevity Uncertainty

How long will you live? Saving "enough" requires knowing your date of death. Without annuity markets, individuals face the risk of either outliving their savings or dying with too much left over.

🏥 Health Cost Uncertainty

Future health expenditures are large, lumpy, and unpredictable. A single major illness can wipe out an entire lifetime of accumulated retirement wealth in a very short period.

📉 Financial Return Uncertainty

Real returns on savings vary enormously over long time horizons. A worker retiring in 2009 (after the financial crisis) faced vastly different outcomes than one retiring in 2007 — even with identical savings behaviour.

🧠 Myopia (Behavioural Bias)

People discount the future heavily ($\delta < 1$) and consistently undersave. Behavioural evidence shows that most individuals, left to their own devices, save far below what rational life-cycle models predict.

The Two-Period Model: Optimal Retirement Saving

To understand the economics of pension saving, we use a simple two-period life-cycle model. The individual lives for two periods: one where they work and earn income, and one where they are retired.

Setup: The individual maximises lifetime utility:

$U = u(c_1) + \delta \cdot u(c_2)$

where $\delta \in [0, 1]$ is the time discount factor (how much you value the future relative to today).

Period Budget Constraints:

Period 1 (Working): You earn wage $w$, consume $c_1$, and save $s$:

$c_1 = w - s$

Period 2 (Retirement): You have no earnings. You consume your savings plus interest:

$c_2 = s \cdot (1 + r)$

Lifetime Budget Constraint: Combining both periods:

$c_1 + \frac{c_2}{1+r} = w$

Variables: $w$ = wage, $s$ = savings, $r$ = interest rate, $c_1, c_2$ = consumption in periods 1 & 2, $\delta$ = time discount factor.

The Life Cycle Model: during working years (0 → R), savings accumulate. After retirement (R → T), wealth is gradually consumed (dissaving).

Case 1: Rational Saver ($\delta = 1$)

When $\delta = 1$, the individual values the future exactly as much as the present. Equal marginal utility in both periods implies $c_1 = c_2$.

Optimal saving:

$s^* = \frac{w}{2}$

The individual saves exactly half their wage — achieving perfectly smooth consumption across both periods. This is the theoretical ideal of the life-cycle model.

Case 2: Myopic Individual ($\delta = 0$)

When $\delta = 0$, the individual does not value the future at all:

$\max [u(c_1) + 0 \cdot u(c_2)] = \max [u(c_1)]$

Result: $c_1 = w$, $c_2 = 0$, $s^* = 0$ — the individual saves nothing and is destitute in retirement.

This creates an externality borne by society: a generation that saves nothing will fall into poverty and depend on public emergency welfare systems.

🎯 Policy Response: If society deems old-age poverty unacceptable, it must either (a) provide ex-post relief (costly, creates moral hazard) or (b) mandate ex-ante saving. This is the primary microeconomic rationale for compulsory pension contributions.

Government Systems: From Market Failure to Political Economy

Since financial markets alone fail to guarantee adequate retirement saving (because of myopia, uncertainty, and adverse selection), the government steps in to force people to save. But this raises several important questions:

  1. One savings rate for everybody?

    Everyone has a different $\delta$ (time preference). The government does not know each individual's $\delta$ — an information problem that makes optimal mandates difficult to design.

  2. How should the government mandate savings?

    Options include: (a) mandating private saving with minimum contribution rates, (b) channelling contributions into a government-managed fund, or (c) using payroll taxes that are immediately redistributed to current retirees — the PAYG model.

  3. Pensions become political.

    Once the government is involved, pension policy becomes deeply political. Pension promises are powerful electoral commitments. Reforms are costly — beneficiary groups are large, organised, and vote in high numbers.

  4. Normative vs. Positive questions.

    Normative: Who should bear retirement risk — individuals, employers, or the state? Positive: How to design a system that is fiscally sustainable and does not distort labour markets, savings rates, or intergenerational equity.

  5. Redistribution within and across generations.

    Pension systems redistribute within a generation (progressive benefit formulas compress lifetime inequality) and across generations (current debt must be serviced by future taxpayers). Political systems handle the former much more easily than the latter.

Six Core Policy Design Questions

Every pension system in the world is defined by how it answers these six fundamental questions:

Q1

Voluntary or Mandatory?

If people are myopic ($\delta$ close to 0) and society refuses to accept old-age poverty, voluntary systems produce under-saving. Mandatory contributions correct this market failure. However, a too-generous mandate forces individuals to contribute more than they would freely choose, generating a welfare loss. Mainstream answer: mandatory for a minimum tier (prevent poverty); voluntary for higher replacement rates.

Q2

Universal or Means-Tested?

Universal: all eligible individuals receive a pension regardless of other income. Better at preventing poverty, stronger political support (middle class defends the system), but more expensive. Means-tested: benefits flow only to those below a threshold. Cheaper, but creates a "pension trap" — saving reduces benefit eligibility, discouraging private saving.

Q3

Who Bears the Risk — DB or DC?

Defined Benefit (DB): the system fixes the benefit formula (e.g. 1.5% × years × reference salary). The sponsor (employer or state) bears investment risk. Defined Contribution (DC): the contribution rate is fixed; the benefit equals whatever the accumulated account can finance. The individual bears all risk.

Q4

Funded or Pay-As-You-Go?

Funded: contributions are invested in financial assets; benefits come from accumulated returns. Return = $r$ (capital market). PAYG: current workers' taxes are directly transferred to current retirees; no fund accumulates. Return = $n + g$ (population growth + wage growth). This is the central question — treated in full in Section 6.3.

Q5

Publicly or Privately Managed?

Public management has lower administration costs (economies of scale, no profit margin) but faces political interference in investment decisions. Private management can deliver higher risk-adjusted returns, but involves significant fees — 1–2% annually can eliminate one-third of lifetime retirement wealth.

Q6

Lump Sum or Annuity?

Annuities provide lifelong income, fully insuring longevity risk — the individual cannot outlive their wealth. However, they are inflexible (no bequest motive). Lump sums offer flexibility and the ability to leave an inheritance, but expose myopic individuals to the risk of rapid dissaving. Evidence from the UK and Australia shows many retirees exhaust funds within a decade.

Pension Systems Are Widely Different

Design choices reflect political, historical, and institutional variation. For example, the Netherlands has 40× larger private pension assets than Belgium. The guaranteed basic pension is 45% of average income in Iceland but only 7% in Hungary.

Unfunded DB (PAYG)

Most public systems worldwide — US Social Security, Czech Republic, Germany (points system), France. Benefits are formula-based; no fund accumulates. Fiscally exposed to ageing demographics.

Funded DB

Traditional employer pensions in US and UK. The sponsor promises a defined benefit and must pre-fund it. Declining rapidly, plagued by insufficient pre-financing and interest-rate sensitivity.

Funded DC

All private pension funds and modern employer plans (US 401(k), Australian Superannuation). Individual accounts; contributions grow at market returns. The worker bears investment and longevity risk.

Unfunded DC (NDC)

Notional accounts in government systems (Sweden 1998, Poland, Latvia, Italy 1995). Payroll taxes yield a fictitious return based on wage-bill growth. Benefits are based on contributions plus this notional return. Combines PAYG funding with DC-style transparency.

Chapter 6.1 — The Pension Problem:

  • People live longer than they earn — some mechanism must transfer income from working years to retirement.
  • The four mechanisms are: storing goods (obsolete), raising children (declining), financial markets (risky), and state pension systems (dominant in advanced economies).
  • The two-period model shows that a rational saver ($\delta = 1$) saves $s^* = w/2$, while a myopic one ($\delta = 0$) saves nothing — creating a market failure that justifies mandatory contributions.
  • Every pension system answers six questions: voluntary/mandatory, universal/means-tested, DB/DC, funded/PAYG, public/private management, and lump sum/annuity.
  • Real-world systems are wildly different — from Sweden's NDC to the US 401(k) to Italy's reformed system — reflecting each country's history and political choices.
