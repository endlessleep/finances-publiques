11.1 — Corporate Decisions & Profit Shifting

Chapter 11: Tax Avoidance — How Taxes Shape Firm Behavior

What Firms Actually Do

A firm is not just a "machine that produces goods." A firm constantly makes strategic decisions, and taxation influences almost all of them. Taxes are not simply a cut of the profits; they directly modify corporate behavior.

The course identifies four major decisions influenced by taxes:

  1. Where and in what form to incorporate? (Location & Legal Structure)
  2. How to finance the firm? (Debt vs. Equity)
  3. How much to invest? (Capital Allocation)
  4. How to redistribute profits? (Dividends vs. Share Buybacks)

For example, if Country A taxes at 30% and Country B at 10%, a multinational corporation (MNC) might choose to place specific activities, declare profits, or locate its legal headquarters in Country B. This is the root cause of international tax competition and the use of tax havens.

Financing Decisions: Debt vs. Equity

Firms need capital to operate, which they can raise through Debt (borrowing) or Equity (issuing shares). The tax system treats these two very differently, creating a major distortion.

Debt Financing

💸 Borrowing

Includes bank loans, bonds, or intra-group borrowing. Firms must pay interest on debt.

Crucial Tax Rule: Interest payments are generally tax-deductible. This lowers the taxable profit, meaning the firm pays less corporate tax.

Equity Financing

📈 Shareholders

Shareholders provide capital in exchange for dividends or capital gains.

Crucial Tax Rule: Dividends are NOT tax-deductible. Therefore, the tax system artificially favors debt over equity.

Debt Shifting

This tax advantage leads to a central mechanism of profit shifting: Debt Shifting.

Imagine a high-tax subsidiary and a tax-haven subsidiary. The tax-haven subsidiary lends money to the high-tax subsidiary. The high-tax subsidiary pays interest, which reduces its local taxable profit, while the interest income flows into the tax haven, where it is lightly taxed.

Investment Decisions & Depreciation

Taxes also influence how much a firm invests in machines, software, and R&D. When a firm buys a machine, it rarely deducts the entire cost immediately. Instead, governments use depreciation rules.

Economic Depreciation

📉 Gradual Deduction

The cost is spread over the asset's useful life (e.g., deducting 10% per year for 10 years). The tax benefit is delayed.

Expensing

⚡ Immediate Deduction

The firm deducts the entire cost in the year of purchase. Because money today is worth more than money tomorrow, expensing is much more advantageous.

Governments often manipulate depreciation rules rather than the statutory corporate tax rate to stimulate investment. Why? Because a general tax cut benefits all profits (even those from past investments), whereas accelerated depreciation or R&D Tax Credits specifically target and reward new investments.

The Three Channels of Profit Shifting

You must absolutely understand the three main channels MNCs use to shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions:

  1. Transfer Mispricing

    MNCs consist of multiple subsidiaries that trade with each other. The prices they charge each other are called transfer prices. If these prices are artificially manipulated, it becomes transfer mispricing.

    The Starbucks Example: A local café sells coffee for €3. Cost is €2. Without optimization, the taxable profit is €1. However, the café pays a €1 "royalty" to a Swiss subsidiary to use the Starbucks brand. The local taxable profit drops to €0, and the €1 profit is shifted to Switzerland.

  2. Debt Shifting

    As explained above, using intra-group loans to strip profits out of high-tax countries via deductible interest payments.

  3. Intangible Assets & Patent Boxes

    Unlike a physical factory, intangible assets (algorithms, brands, patents, logos) are extremely easy to move legally. Firms locate these assets in tax havens or countries with a Patent Box (a highly advantageous tax regime for IP revenue). Other subsidiaries then pay massive royalties to the tax haven.

Paper Profit Shifting & The Tax Gap

💡 Crucial Concept: Paper Profit Shifting. The core issue is that profits move, but the real economic activity does not. A subsidiary in Bermuda might declare billions in profit with only 20 employees and no physical factories. The profits on paper completely disconnect from the actual productive substance.

Certain countries act as major hubs (e.g., Ireland). They offer low taxation and favorable rules for IP, attracting massive amounts of corporate profits. This heavily distorts macroeconomic statistics, making these countries look artificially wealthy compared to their true domestic activity.

The Tax Gap

The difference between what governments should theoretically collect and what they actually collect. Estimates suggest hundreds of billions of dollars are lost globally.

While developed nations lose significant revenue in absolute terms, developing countries are often the most vulnerable. They rely more heavily on corporate tax revenue and lack the administrative resources to fight aggressive optimization, worsening global inequality.

Corporate Tax Incidence & Avoidance vs. Evasion

Who really pays the corporate tax? It is not just the shareholders. The burden (incidence) can fall on:

  • Workers: If taxes reduce investment, productivity falls, leading to lower wages.
  • Consumers: Firms may pass the tax burden on by raising prices.

Measuring this empirically is extremely difficult because many other macroeconomic factors affect wages and prices.

Tax Avoidance vs. Tax Evasion

Tax Avoidance

⚖️ Legal Optimization

Using legal loopholes, complex structures, and the mismatch between national systems to minimize tax. This is often an aggressive but legal "gray area".

Tax Evasion

🚨 Illegal Fraud

Deliberately hiding income, using fake documents, or maintaining undeclared offshore accounts.

MNCs have a massive strategic advantage over domestic firms because they can exploit tax avoidance across multiple jurisdictions—something local businesses simply cannot do. This leads to empirical phenomena like Bunching, where firms artificially report profits just below a threshold where a higher tax rate would apply.
