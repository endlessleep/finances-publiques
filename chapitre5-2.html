5.2 — Public Finance Management (PFM)

Chapter 5: Central and Local Government Public Finance

The Three Objectives (Layers) of PFM

Public Finance Management (PFM) is the technical framework used to ensure public funds are handled effectively. It can be understood through three distinct, yet overlapping objectives:

1. Fiscal Discipline

Goal: Economy. Ensuring that total spending stays within the budget limits and avoids excessive deficits. This is the foundational layer — without it, all other objectives are meaningless. A government that cannot control its spending will eventually face inflation, currency crises, or default.

2. Purposeful Allocation

Goal: Effectiveness. It is not enough to spend within the ceiling — the money must go to the right priorities. This means directing resources toward areas with the highest social return (e.g., shifting funds from bureaucratic overhead to education or green infrastructure).

3. Value for Money

Goal: Efficiency. Even if resources go to the right areas, they might still be wasted through poor management. This layer asks: "for every euro spent, are we getting the maximum possible outcome?" It requires constant measurement and evaluation.

💡 How the Layers Connect: Think of it as a pyramid. Discipline is the base (can we afford it?), Allocation is the middle (are we spending on the right things?), and Value for Money is the top (are we spending well?). A country can satisfy Layer 1 while failing Layer 3 — for instance, if it respects the deficit ceiling but wastes resources on ineffective programs.

Layer 1: Fiscal Discipline (Stability)

Fiscal discipline is the foundation of economic stability. Without it, a country risks inflation, currency crises, or debt defaults.

The Tools of Discipline: Fiscal discipline does not happen by good intentions alone. It requires institutional mechanisms that constrain political behavior:

  • Fiscal Rules: These are legally binding limits on deficits or debt levels. For example, the EU's Stability and Growth Pact imposes a 3% deficit-to-GDP limit and a 60% debt-to-GDP ceiling. The idea is to tie politicians' hands before they are tempted to overspend for electoral gain.
  • Fiscal Council: Independent bodies (like the Czech Fiscal Council or the UK's OBR) that monitor government promises and publicly assess whether fiscal forecasts are realistic. They act as a credibility anchor — if the government's projections are too optimistic, the Council says so publicly.
  • Top-Down Budgeting: Instead of letting each ministry demand what it wants and then negotiating upwards, the central government first sets a hard spending ceiling for the total budget. Only then do individual ministries compete for their share within that fixed envelope. This prevents "bottom-up" spending spirals.
🏮 Fiscal Risks: Modern PFM requires constant "Fiscal Risk Analysis" — assessing how natural disasters, wars, or banking collapses might blow a hole in the budget.

Layer 2: Purposeful Allocation (Strategic)

It is not enough to stay within the budget; the money must be spent on the right things. This is the strategic layer of public finance.

Programme Budgeting: Instead of giving a ministry money for "salaries" or "rent", the government funds specific programs (e.g., "Reduction of Child Poverty"). Each program must have clear objectives and be connected to a long-term strategy.

Strategic Planning: To achieve purposeful allocation, governments use a Medium-Term Budgetary Framework (MTBF). This ensures that a decision made today (like building a hospital) takes into account the costs of running it for the next 4-5 years, not just the construction cost this year.

Layer 3: Value for Money (Efficiency)

This final layer asks: "Are we using our resources efficiently?" It is the most technically complex objective to achieve.

Performance Budgeting

Budgets are linked to Key Performance Indicators (KPIs). If a school receives more money, we expect to see an improvement in student grades or graduation rates.

Spending Reviews

A systematic analysis of existing spending to identify "waste" or "inefficiency". These are not just "budget cuts"; they are targeted removals of programs that no longer work.

International Benchmarks: How do leading countries implement Value for Money?

  • Canada: Uses "Departmental Plans" where each ministry must publicly show citizens exactly what each dollar achieves in terms of measurable outcomes. This creates transparency and public accountability.
  • Austria: Integrates Wirkungsziele (Impact Goals) directly into the constitutional budget law. Every ministry must define concrete outcome targets alongside its spending request — it is impossible to request a budget without specifying what it will achieve.
  • The Netherlands: Famous for its rigorous "Spending Reviews" that systematically ask: "If we started from scratch, would we still fund this program?" These reviews focus on long-term structural efficiency rather than short-term budget cuts.

Chapter 5.2 — The Three Layers:

  • Layer 1 (Discipline): Use fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils, and top-down budgeting to ensure the government does not spend beyond its means. This is the necessary condition.
  • Layer 2 (Allocation): Use programme budgeting and Medium-Term Budgetary Frameworks (MTBF) to ensure money flows toward the areas with the highest social return. This is the strategic condition.
  • Layer 3 (Efficiency): Use performance budgeting, KPIs, and spending reviews to ensure every euro produces measurable results. This is the sufficient condition — the difference between spending money and achieving outcomes.
  • A country can satisfy one layer while failing another. The goal of modern PFM is to satisfy all three simultaneously.
