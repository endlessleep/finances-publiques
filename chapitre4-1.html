4.1 — Public Goods

Chapter 4: Public Goods & Externalities

Market Failures: An Overview

A market failure happens when the market does not allocate resources efficiently. Even if everyone acts rationally, the total welfare of society is not maximized.

Main sources of market failure: The following are the key situations where markets, left to themselves, fail to deliver an efficient outcome:

  • Imperfect competition: When firms have market power (monopoly, oligopoly), they can set prices above marginal cost, leading to lower output and higher prices than the competitive equilibrium.
  • Incomplete markets: Some goods, services, or risks are simply not covered by private markets — for instance, insurance against very rare catastrophic events, or credit for the poorest households.
  • Imperfect information: Markets assume that buyers and sellers have full information. In reality, asymmetric information leads to adverse selection (bad products drive out good ones) and moral hazard (people take more risks when insured).
  • Public goods: Goods that are non-excludable and non-rival — the market cannot charge for them, so the private sector won't provide them.
  • Externalities: Effects on third parties that are not reflected in market prices — pollution, education spillovers, etc.

In this chapter, we focus specifically on Public Goods and Externalities.

Taxonomy of Goods

To understand why markets fail for certain goods, we use two criteria:

Rivalry

If one person consumes a good, does it reduce the amount available for others?

Excludability

Is it possible to prevent someone who doesn't pay from using the good?

Pureness of Public Goods

In theory, we distinguish between:

  • Pure Public Goods: Perfectly non-rival and non-excludable (e.g., national defense).
  • Impure Public Goods: Goods that are mostly non-rival but can suffer from congestion (e.g., a highway that becomes rival when there's too much traffic).

The Public Goods Problem

Why is there a problem with public goods? It comes down to why the private sector often fails to provide them.

Private Failure

The Incentive Problem

Non-excludability: Since it's impossible to stop people from using the good without paying, private firms have no incentive to produce it. This leads to undersupply.

Non-rivalry: The marginal cost of an extra user is zero. This can lead to underconsumption if a price is charged.

Public Solution

The Government's Role

The government can step in and provide these goods using two types of funding:

  • User fees: Paying based on use (the benefit principle).
  • Taxation: Paying based on wealth (the ability-to-pay principle).

Equity-Efficiency Trade-offs: Sometimes the government provides Merit Goods (like education or healthcare) even if they are excludable, because society deems them essential for equity.

🞰 The Free-Rider Problem: Since no one can be excluded from using a public good, everyone has an incentive to not pay while still benefiting. Rational agents hope that others will contribute, creating a collective action problem that makes voluntary funding almost impossible. This is why taxation — enforced, mandatory contribution — is the standard solution.

Experiment: The Public Goods Game

This experiment shows the conflict between individual interest and the common good.

The Setup:

  • Everyone has 10 tokens.
  • You secretly choose how many to put into a "public pot".
  • The pot is multiplied (e.g., by 1.5) and split equally between everyone.

The Result: Individually, the best choice is to contribute 0. But if everyone contributes 0, the whole group loses. If everyone contributes 10, everyone wins big.

Typical Lab Findings (Isaac, Walker & Thomas 1984; Ledyard 1995):
1. Initial Cooperation: Average contributions are 40–60% of endowment. People are driven by altruism, reciprocity, and social norms.
2. Stability Issues: With repetition, contributions decline steadily toward zero as cooperators observe free-riders and stop trusting the group.
3. The Punishment Effect (Fehr & Gächter 2000): Being able to "pay to punish" free-riders sustains high cooperation.

Policy Analogy: Voluntary contributions fail. Punishment (enforcement) via taxation is the only way to sustain cooperation and prevent collapse.

The Optimal Quantity

How much of a public good should the government provide? Since there are no market prices, we have to find the "efficient" level manually.

Demand (MWTP)

The Marginal Willingness to Pay. For public goods, we sum demand vertically (adding up how much every person values the same unit).

Supply (MC)

The Marginal Cost of providing the good, usually determined through cost-benefit analysis and the government budget.

🎯 Efficiency Condition: Marginal Willingness to Pay = Marginal Cost. In public finance, this is decided through the political process rather than market competition.

Public Goods — The Essential Logic:

  • Public goods are non-rival (my consumption doesn't reduce yours) and non-excludable (you can't stop people from benefiting without paying).
  • This combination creates a free-rider problem: everyone wants to benefit, but no one wants to pay. The private sector therefore produces too little.
  • The government solves this through mandatory taxation — forcing everyone to contribute removes the free-rider incentive.
  • The efficient quantity is found where the total social benefit (sum of all individuals' willingness to pay) equals the marginal cost of provision.
