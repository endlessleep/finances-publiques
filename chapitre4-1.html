4.1 — Public Goods

Chapter 4: Public Goods & Externalities

Market Failures: An Overview

A market failure happens when the free market makes bad choices and wastes resources. Even if everyone acts smartly for themselves, the total happiness and wealth of society is not maximized.

Main reasons for market failure: These are the key situations where free markets fail to do a good job and need help:

  • Imperfect competition: When a few companies have too much power (like a monopoly), they can unfairly raise prices, making people pay more for less.
  • Incomplete markets: Some important services are simply not sold by private companies — for example, insurance against huge rare disasters, or cheap bank loans for very poor families.
  • Imperfect information: Markets only work when buyers and sellers know the whole truth. But usually, one side hides important secrets (like selling a broken used car), which destroys trust.
  • Public goods: Things that are non-excludable and non-rival — like streetlights. Since companies can't force people to pay for looking at a streetlight, they refuse to build them.
  • Externalities: When a private action accidentally hurts or helps innocent bystanders — like a factory blowing smoke onto a neighbor's house.

In this chapter, we will look closely at Public Goods and Externalities.

Pure vs. Impure Public Goods

In real life, we separate these into two groups:

  • Pure Public Goods: 100% non-rival and 100% non-excludable (like the country's military defense).
  • Impure Public Goods: Things that are mostly non-rival but can break down due to crowding (like a free highway that becomes a nightmare during rush hour).

The Public Goods Problem

Why are public goods a problem? Because private businesses refuse to create them.

Market Failure

The Motivation Problem

Non-excludability: Because businessmen cannot stop people from using the good safely for free, they have zero motivation to build it. This leads to not enough being built.

Non-rivalry: Adding one more user costs the business absolutely nothing ($0). If they charge money, they block people unnecessarily, leading to under-usage.

The State Solution

The Government's Job

The government must step in and buy these goods for society using two methods:

  • User fees: You only pay when you use it (like a toll road). This is the benefit principle.
  • Taxes: Rich people pay more, poor pay less. This is the ability-to-pay principle.

Fairness over Money: Sometimes the government forces everyone to pay for Merit Goods (like free hospitals or schools) because society believes everyone deserves them, even poor people.

🞰 The Free-Rider Problem: Because no one can be stopped from enjoying a public good (like a clean park), every selfish person wants to secretly not pay and let other people pay for them. Everyone hopes their neighbor will open their wallet first. Because of this sneaky cheating, asking for voluntary donations always fails. This is exactly why mandatory government taxes are required to build a civilized country.

Game: The Public Goods Experiment

This famous psychology game proves the harsh battle between greedy individuals and trusting groups.

The Game Rules:

  • Every student is given 10 tokens.
  • You secretly choose how many tokens to throw into a "team pot".
  • The teacher magically multiplies the team pot by 1.5, and gives equal money back to everyone, even those who gave nothing.

The Result: For one greedy person, the smartest trick is to give 0 and steal the multiplied team money. But if everyone acts greedy and gives 0, the game breaks and everyone stays poor. If everyone bravely gives 10, everyone becomes incredibly rich together.

Actual Lab Results (Isaac, Walker & Thomas 1984; Ledyard 1995):
1. Trust at the Start: People usually donate about half their money (40-60%) in the first round because humans are naturally kind and trusting.
2. Trust Dies Quickly: Over many rounds, donations crash down to zero because the kind people realize the greedy people are cheating them, so they stop paying too.
3. The Power of Punishment (Fehr & Gächter 2000): If the game allows players to pay a small fee to "hurt" the cheating free-riders, donations suddenly jump to 100% and stay there.

Real World Lesson: Asking nicely for money fails. Strict laws and punishing tax evaders is the only way a society can survive and build nice things.

Calculating the Perfect Amount

How many streetlights or tanks should the government buy? Because there are no normal price tags, politicians must calculate the "efficient" sweet spot manually.

Desire (MWTP)

The Marginal Willingness to Pay. Because everyone can look at the same streetlight at once, we add up the desire of the citizens vertically (combining everyone’s happiness for one light).

Cost (MC)

The Marginal Cost of buying the object, which is figured out through standard government accounting.

🎯 The Perfect Balance rule: Total Desire (Willingness to Pay) = Marginal Cost. In real life, this answer isn't found perfectly in a textbook; it is fought over through voting and elections.

Public Goods — The Core Lesson:

  • Public goods are non-rival (sharing doesn't ruin it) and non-excludable (you can't block free-loaders).
  • This creates the toxic free-rider problem: everyone demands the item, but nobody wants to be the stupid person who pays. Private companies run away.
  • The state steps in with forced taxes to solve the cheating and buy the item for everyone objectively.
  • The perfect amount to build is found when the combined happiness of all citizens equals the factory cost of the item.
